Dragon: carry the margins with the digits

Ordinary long division can expose decimal digits exactly, but it does not know when enough digits have been printed. Dragon carries the distance to both parsing boundaries through the same recurrence, allowing the loop itself to prove when it may stop.

4.1 · Output contract

Enough digits to recover the input, and no more

Dragon produces a decimal that a correctly rounded reader maps back to the original floating-point value. It stops at the smallest significant-digit count for which this is possible and chooses the closer of the two possible last digits.

This is the transition from Coonen’s requested-width conversion to free-format conversion. The caller no longer supplies N. The algorithm determines the number of digits from the input’s exact neighborhood.

RequirementMeaning in this chapter
RecoveryParsing the decimal under round-to-nearest, ties-to-even recovers the original binary64 bits.
ShortestNo decimal with fewer significant digits has the recovery property. This does not yet choose fixed versus scientific character layout.
ClosestIf the last decimal position admits two recovering candidates, choose the one nearest the exact binary value, with an even last digit for an exact decimal tie.

Steele and White present the construction for arbitrary internal and external radices and recommend Dragon4 for general runtime use. The executable specialization here fixes the internal format to IEEE binary64, the output radix to ten, and the parser rule to IEEE round-to-nearest, ties-to-even. The original paper is available as a scanned PDF.

4.2 · The familiar recurrence

Generate only the digits that settle the answer

Dragon needs a decimal candidate inside the parsing interval, but it does not know in advance how many digits that requires. Its strategy is to reveal the decimal expansion from left to right. After each new digit it asks whether the prefix is already accurate enough to recover the input. If not, it reveals one more digit.

The mechanism is ordinary long division. Consider the exact fraction 1/3. Multiplying it by ten gives 10/3, or 3 + 1/3. The whole part, 3, is the next decimal digit. The fractional part left over is again 1/3, so repeating the same operation produces another 3:

1/3  →  multiply by 10  →  3 + 1/3.

The same operation works for any exact remainder. Write the still-unprinted fraction as R/S, with R smaller than S. Multiplication by ten brings the next decimal digit into the whole-number part. Integer division produces that digit, while the division remainder becomes the new unprinted fraction.

Only now is the compact implementation equation useful:

10R = qS + r,    0 ≤ q ≤ 9,    0 ≤ r < S.

Here q is the next decimal digit. The fraction r/S is everything still unprinted. In the 1/3 example, R = 1 and S = 3, so 10 = 3·3 + 1: the digit is 3 and the new remainder is again 1.

That is exactly the problem. Long division knows the digits of the exact value, but shortest output often ends before the exact expansion ends. Binary64(1/3) is not the real number 1/3, and its shortest recovering decimal is 0.3333333333333333. A stopping rule must be derived from binary64’s finite spacing, not from the decimal remainder becoming zero.

4.3 · The central representation

Measure how much error the parser will forgive

Return first to the number line, before choosing any integer representation. The selected double v lies between a lower and an upper parsing boundary. A decimal candidate need not equal v; it only needs to remain between those boundaries.

After some digits have been emitted, truncating the remaining expansion gives the decimal candidate immediately below v. The unprinted remainder is exactly the distance from that lower candidate to v. If this distance fits within the room to the lower boundary, the truncated candidate recovers the input. The adjacent decimal at the same precision lies one decimal step above it; if its distance back to v fits within the upper room, that candidate recovers the input.

These two distances are the stopping information missing from ordinary long division. Dragon calls their integer representations M⁻ and M⁺. The minus and plus signs refer to the lower and upper side of v; they are not arithmetic operations performed by the loop.

The names remain attached to roles. R is the exact unprinted remainder, S is its denominator, and M⁻/M⁺ are the permitted lower/upper errors in the same integer units. Their numerical values change as digits are emitted, but these meanings do not.

4.4 · Locate the leading digit

Only now turn the geometry into integers

The preceding stopping argument did not require scaling. Scaling is the implementation step that lets Dragon perform every comparison exactly with integers.

First, Dragon determines the scientific exponent k for which v·10−k lies in [1,10). This places the leading decimal digit in the integer part. The value and both boundaries receive exactly the same power-of-ten scale, so no point moves relative to the others. In the executable version, rational comparisons correct the initial exponent estimate; a floating-point logarithm is never trusted as the final decision.

Next, it chooses one common denominator S. The selected value becomes R/S. Its distance to the lower boundary becomes M⁻/S, and its distance to the upper boundary becomes M⁺/S:

M⁻/S = v − lower boundary,    M⁺/S = upper boundary − v.

The four integers may be large, but every later operation is elementary: comparison, addition or subtraction, multiplication by ten, and a division whose quotient is known to be one decimal digit.

  1. construct the exact center and its lower and upper midpoint boundaries
  2. choose decimal exponent k; scale center and margins to integers R, S, M−, M+
  3. q ← floor(R / S); R ← R mod S; append digit q
  4. low ← lower candidate is inside; high ← upper candidate is inside
  5. if low or high, choose the permitted nearer candidate and stop
  6. R ← 10R; M− ← 10M−; M+ ← 10M+; repeat

4.5 · The stopping tests

Test the two possible last digits separately

At any chosen number of digits, only two adjacent decimals can be nearest to the exact value. The first is obtained by keeping the digits generated so far and discarding the remainder. The second is one unit higher in the final decimal place.

After integer division extracts a digit, write r for the new remainder. This is the distance from the lower candidate up to the exact value, expressed in the common integer units. The complete decimal step has size S, so S − r is the distance from the exact value up to the upper candidate.

Dragon now compares those distances with the room available on the corresponding side. If the lower distance is smaller than the lower margin, the lower candidate is inside. If the upper distance is smaller than the upper margin, the upper candidate is inside. In the compact notation used by implementations:

lower candidate is admissible when r < M⁻;
upper candidate is admissible when S − r < M⁺.

The inequalities become non-strict when the corresponding midpoint endpoint belongs to the selected binary64 value. For round-to-nearest, ties-to-even, both endpoints are included when the selected integer significand is even and excluded when it is odd.

lowhighAction
falsefalseNeither candidate recovers the input. Multiply the remainder and both margins by ten and generate another digit.
truefalseThe lower candidate recovers; emit the current last digit and stop.
falsetrueThe upper candidate recovers; increase the last digit and stop.
truetrueBoth recover. Compare 2r with S to choose the candidate nearer v; choose an even last digit if they are exactly equidistant.

Testing low and high independently is essential at an exponent transition, where the two margins can differ by a factor of two. A symmetric “remainder is below one half” rounding test cannot replace them.

4.6 · Executable digit generation

Follow Dragon for binary64(1/3)

The instrument is generated from the exact BigInt implementation used by the tests. The upper lane retains every digit already generated. The lower lane magnifies the current decimal cell, keeping the selected value, both adjacent decimals, and the carried interval in one stable geometry. Repeated 3s are computed states, not prewritten frames.

For the first fifteen digits, neither adjacent decimal lies inside the parsing interval. At digit sixteen, 0.3333333333333333 enters through the lower side. The loop stops although the exact binary64 expansion still has many nonzero digits.

4.7 · Structural cases

Some complications belong to the number format, not to Dragon

Equal spacing is the ordinary case, but an exponent transition can place the predecessor twice as close as the successor. Endpoint ownership comes from the parser’s ties-to-even rule. These facts affect every shortest conversion algorithm; Dragon merely records them as two separate margins and strict or non-strict comparisons.

The binary64 primer’s exponent-transition instrument shows the closer predecessor directly, and the parsing-geometry chapter shows how endpoint ownership changes under ties-to-even. They are format facts rather than extra Dragon steps, so they are not replayed here as an algorithm trace.

The remaining special inputs are best separated from the digit loop. Signed zero, infinities, and NaNs are classified before conversion. Negative finite values use the positive magnitude path and receive a sign afterward. Subnormals still participate in the same interval construction; the binary64 primer explains why their effective precision changes near zero. The executable corpus covers the normal/subnormal transition and both ends of the finite range.

4.8 · Why the recurrence is sufficient

The same multiplication advances digits and proof

Three invariants carry the argument:

  1. The emitted prefix plus the exact remainder R/S denotes the original value at the current decimal position.
  2. M⁻/S and M⁺/S denote the exact distances from that value to the two parsing boundaries at the same position.
  3. Multiplying R, M⁻, and M⁺ by ten changes only the decimal position. It preserves all three represented real quantities relative to the next digit cell.

If neither stopping test succeeds, no decimal with the current number of significant digits can recover the input: the only two adjacent candidates both lie outside the interval. When a test first succeeds, its candidate is therefore shortest. If both succeed, comparing the exact remainder with half the denominator chooses the closest shortest candidate.

The margins grow by a factor of ten on every continuation. The normalized remainder remains below the denominator. Consequently one of the margins must eventually reach an adjacent candidate, so the loop terminates. Binary64 requires at most 17 significant decimal digits for recovery.

4.9 · Keeping exactness, removing work

Dragon became a baseline, not a final implementation shape

David Gay’s dtoa retained the exact stopping logic while adding common-case floating-point estimates, cached powers, and carefully limited big-integer work. Burger and Dybvig reorganized scaling and digit generation to make exact free- and fixed-format conversion substantially faster. Their 1996 paper also gives a more direct correctness development of the free-format loop.

These descendants remain in the Dragon family because they improve the exact machinery without replacing its central interval argument. They still deserve separate executable subsections within that family. The present draft establishes their shared conceptual object: a remainder and two exact margins that advance together. Grisu retains the same geometry while replacing unbounded integers with a small approximate floating-point representation and a proof that decides whether the approximation is safe.

4.10 · Evidence and implementation status

The exact teaching path is executable

The repository now contains a readable BigInt implementation of the binary64-to-decimal digit loop. It uses a different search strategy from the grid-search oracle: Dragon emits digits and tests two margins, while the oracle enumerates admissible coefficients independently at each significant-digit count.

ImplementedExact boundary construction; separate lower and upper margins; decimal normalization; digit-by-digit remainder recurrence; endpoint-aware stopping; closest-candidate selection; trace events.

Current corpusNamed ordinary and difficult cases, transition-adjacent values, subnormals, maximum finite values, carry-sensitive inputs, negative values, and 1,500 deterministic binary64 bit patterns compared with the exact grid-search oracle.

Still requiredExhaustive binary32 instantiation; upstream Dragon/dtoa hard cases; a pinned native Dragon4 or dtoa wrapper; readable implementations of Gay and Burger–Dybvig fast paths.

Claim boundaryThe current evidence supports the exact teaching specialization. It is not yet a correctness claim for any native optimized descendant.
