The shape of the binary64 number line

Binary64 values form a regular grid only while the exponent remains fixed. This chapter develops the changing spacing, the exceptional midpoint geometry at powers of two, and the gradual transition to subnormal values.

1.1 · Representation

Three fields select one exact value

A finite binary64 datum consists of a sign, an eleven-bit exponent field, and a 52-bit fraction field. For normal values, those fields denote one integer significand multiplied by one power of two.

The strip shows the bits of the binary64 value obtained by parsing 0.3. The sign occupies one bit. The stored exponent occupies the next eleven and is biased by 1023, so the bit pattern 1021 denotes the exponent −2. The remaining 52 bits store the fractional part of the significand. A normal value has an additional leading 1 that is understood rather than stored.

v = (−1)s · (252 + fraction) · 2E−1023−52.

The apparently fractional quantity is therefore an integer on a scaled grid. Holding the exponent field E fixed and increasing the fraction by one moves to the next grid point. The distance between adjacent values in that region is

ulp = 2E−1023−52.

One ulp is one local step. Near 1, adjacent binary64 values in the upper binade differ by 2−52, so that distance is one ulp there. Moving the fraction field by one moves the value by one ulp. “Half an ulp” means half of this local gap; it is not one fixed real number throughout the format.

The conversion chapters use an integer significand and an explicit power of two because that form makes both the exact value and its local spacing available without floating-point arithmetic. When a later chapter uses a decimal ulp, it means the analogous gap between adjacent decimal results at the chosen precision—for example, 0.00001 between five-digit values near 0.33333.

1.2 · Constant-spacing regions

A binade is one regular part of the grid

For a fixed integer e, the positive normal values in the half-open interval [2e, 2e+1) have the same exponent and the same spacing, 2e−52. This interval is called a binade. Each normal binade contains 252 distinct values, so relative precision remains essentially constant while absolute spacing grows with magnitude.

Crossing from one binade to the next increments the exponent and resets the fraction field. The first gap in the new binade is twice a gap in the preceding binade. Conversely, moving downward across a positive power of two enters a grid with twice as many points per unit of the real line.

Reading the figures. A tick denotes a representable binary64 value. Tick positions use exact differences from the selected value. A bracket denotes the real distance between adjacent ticks; it is not a count of bit patterns.

1.3 · The boundary at 1

The spacing changes at an exact power of two

The value 1 is the first point in the binade [1,2). Its successor is 1 + 2−52. Its predecessor belongs to [½,1) and is 1 − 2−53. Thus the predecessor is only half as far from 1 as the successor. The computed figure makes the two local units visible.

The unequal gaps also make the two parsing midpoints unequal in their distance from 1. The lower midpoint lies 2−54 below 1; the upper midpoint lies 2−53 above it. A shortest printer that represents its acceptable interval by one symmetric radius would therefore be wrong at these inputs. Algorithms either construct both endpoints, carry distinct lower and upper margins, or enter a specialized shorter-interval case.

1.4 · The lower end

Subnormal values preserve a final fixed spacing

If the exponent field is zero, binary64 omits the implicit leading one. A positive subnormal with fraction field f denotes

v = f · 2−1074,    1 ≤ f < 252.

The positive subnormals therefore form a uniformly spaced continuation from zero. The smallest positive value is 2−1074; the next is twice that value; and so forth. After 252−1 subnormal points, the smallest normal value 2−1022 continues with the same spacing. This mechanism is called gradual underflow.

Subnormals have fewer significant nonzero bits than normal values near the same scale. They do not, however, require a different real-number interpretation: they remain exact integer multiples of a power of two. Many implementations normalize the significand internally and then reuse the main conversion path. A chapter will call out subnormal handling only when it changes the algorithm rather than merely its decoding step.

1.5 · What later chapters may assume

One grid, with two structural qualifications

For most inputs, neighboring values are equally spaced and the acceptable round-trip interval is symmetric. At a power-of-two transition the lower and upper spacing differ. Near zero, the exponent no longer decreases and the subnormal significand supplies gradual underflow. These are properties of binary64 itself, independent of any printing algorithm.
