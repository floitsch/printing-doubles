Ryū: make the decimal grid coarser until it would miss

Ryū first places the complete parsing interval on a fine decimal integer grid. It then groups grid cells by ten, removing one decimal digit of resolution each time. The last grid that still contains an admissible integer determines the shortest result.

7.1 · The organizing change

Begin at a fine decimal grid and move outward

Dragon asks for the next digit. Ryū asks a different question: can ten adjacent decimal cells be treated as one larger cell without losing every decimal that recovers the input?

The instrument below begins with more decimal resolution than the final answer needs. Move the resolution slider toward the right. The upper figure shows the exact parsing interval on the current decimal grid. The cells below show how ten positions at the current resolution become one position on the next grid.

For 0.3, the first two coarseward moves greatly reduce the set of possible integers. The interval then contains exactly one integer ending in many zeros. Each subsequent grouping removes one of those zeros without changing the represented decimal. At the last valid grid, the integer 3 denotes 3×10−1. Move the slider one position farther: the whole-number grid contains no admissible integer.

This empty grid is the proof of shortestness. A decimal with fewer significant digits would lie on that grid or an even coarser one. Since none can enter the parsing interval, the preceding candidate cannot be shortened further.

The geometry has not changed. A decimal recovers the selected binary64 value precisely when its exact real value lies between the two parsing boundaries. What changed is the direction of work: Ryū first obtains enough decimal information, then removes resolution from the right.

7.2 · One exponent, three numerators

Two guard bits make the midpoints integral

A midpoint normally sits halfway between adjacent binary values. At a power of two, the lower midpoint can be only one quarter of the upper spacing from the stored value. Carrying fractions through every later multiplication would complicate the integer program. Ryū instead changes the coordinate scale before it begins.

The upper and lower rows describe the same real positions. The lower ruler merely has four times as many coordinate units. In the stored representation, this is achieved by appending two zero bits to the significand and reducing the exponent by two. Let the positive finite input be m2 × 2e:

m2 × 2e = (4m2) × 2e−2.

The center numerator is now 4m2. In the usual equally spaced case the two midpoint numerators are two units below and above it. At an exact power of two the predecessor is closer, so the lower midpoint is only one unit below. The large value of m2 is incidental; the visual fact is the small local pattern −2, 0, +2 or −1, 0, +2.

Evenness decides whether exact boundary values are accepted. Ryū records this as acceptBounds. The later trailing-zero tests are not peripheral optimizations: they determine whether a scaled integer denotes an exact endpoint and hence whether that endpoint may be retained.

7.3 · Change the radix

Project the complete interval onto a decimal grid

Suppose the common binary form is M × 2e2. Ryū chooses a decimal exponent e10 and needs the integer part of

M × 2e₂ / 10e₁₀.

The chosen exponent leaves roughly seventeen or eighteen decimal digits. This is fine enough to distinguish every binary64 rounding interval, but still small enough that the projected center and boundaries fit in 64 bits. The exact teaching implementation below computes the rational quotient directly. Production Ryū obtains the same significant bits with bounded integer arithmetic.

For 0.3, the first step is 10−18. The stored binary value and its boundaries project to integers near 300000000000000000. The long integer is not intended as something the reader should memorize; it says only that the first grid distinguishes positions eighteen places after the decimal point. The resolution laboratory keeps the exponent and the surviving count visible while exact integers remain available through inspection.

7.4 · Why powers of five appear

A decimal power is a binary shift plus a power of five

Hardware shifts already provide multiplication or division by powers of two. The remaining part of a decimal scale is a power of five:

10q = 2q5q.

If the binary exponent is negative, multiplication by 5i supplies the decimal numerator before a shift discards the unwanted fractional bits. If the binary exponent is nonnegative, Ryū instead multiplies by a fixed-point reciprocal of 5q. The reciprocal is stored with enough extra bits that taking the indicated high part of the product gives the required integer projection.

This is the same division-to-multiplication transformation introduced in the floating-point reminder, but here its error is not merely expected to be small. The paper derives the number of retained bits and the direction of rounding so that the integer result is known exactly. A table entry is therefore a compact proof artifact: it is an integer enclosure of a particular power of five or its reciprocal.

Full and size-optimized tables. The reference implementation can store split constants for every required power, or compute them from a smaller representation. This changes code and read-only data size, not the conversion theorem or the decimal selected.

7.5 · Fixed-width arithmetic

Keep the useful half of a wide product

A binary64 significand fits in 53 bits. A cached scale occupies roughly 128 bits. Their mathematical product is wider than a machine word, but the algorithm asks only for a known window of its bits.

The diagram is deliberately schematic: the number of drawn columns is reduced so the product remains legible. In the real algorithm, the chosen power of five and shift place precisely the quotient bits required for the current exponent in the retained region. On machines with a 64×64→128 operation, a small number of partial products supplies that region directly. Portable implementations split each operand into high and low 32-bit halves and combine the four 32×32 products with carries.

The shift count is part of the scale construction. After multiplication, shifting by that count produces the three projected integers for the lower boundary, center, and upper boundary. Ryū’s mulShiftAll64 shares most of the work between the three values: their numerators differ by only a few units, so three unrelated wide products would discard useful structure.

No binary floating-point multiplication is used. “Fixed width” does not mean “approximate and hope”; it means that the proof establishes in advance exactly which product bits can influence the quotient and retains all of them.

7.6 · The shortestness loop

Remove a digit only while an admissible integer survives

Ryū maintains an integer interval and a center approximation on the current decimal grid. Dividing all three by ten asks what would happen if the final printed digit were removed. If the divided upper endpoint is no greater than the divided lower endpoint, the coarser interval is empty after the endpoint rules are applied. Removal stops.

The common path removes two digits at once when division by 100 already leaves room, then continues one digit at a time. This is only a loop optimization. The explanatory implementation deliberately performs one division per state so the invariant remains visible.

  1. decode m2 × 2^e; express center and midpoint boundaries with two guard bits
  2. choose 10^e10; project all three values to bounded integers
  3. while the interval remains nonempty after division by ten, remove one digit
  4. choose the closest integer on the last nonempty grid; apply endpoint and tie rules

7.7 · Information removed with the digits

One discarded digit is sometimes not enough

In the common case, the last removed digit tells whether the retained center should be incremented. A value below 5 rounds down; a value above 5 rounds up. Exact fives require more information: digits removed earlier may have been nonzero, and the projected center may lie exactly on a decimal halfway point.

Ryū therefore tracks whether the center still consists entirely of trailing zeros and whether a boundary projection was exact. If the discarded suffix is exactly 5000… and the retained integer is even, the usual round-to-even rule keeps it. If the retained integer coincides with an excluded lower endpoint, it must instead be incremented even when ordinary rounding would keep it.

These booleans explain several compact-looking branches in the reference source. They carry facts about exact divisibility by powers of two and five. Without them, an implementation may still pass millions of random tests while failing on the deliberately sparse set of exact boundaries and decimal ties.

7.8 · Correctness structure

The optimized program refines an exact integer algorithm

The proof can be separated into four claims. First, the guarded numerators describe the exact center and midpoint boundaries, including the shorter lower interval at powers of two. Second, the power-of-five multiplication and shift produce the required integer projections. Third, each successful division by ten is equivalent to moving to a coarser decimal grid that still intersects the parsing interval. Fourth, the final rounding and endpoint tests select the closest admissible integer on the first grid for which one more removal is impossible.

The third claim proves shortestness: every decimal with fewer significant digits lies on a still coarser grid, and the immediately coarser grid is already empty. The fourth proves the closest part of the output contract. Parsing recovery follows because the chosen integer is retained only when it lies inside the exact rounding interval.

The chapter follows Ulf Adams’s PLDI paper and the author’s reference implementation. The repository includes C, Java, table-generation programs, exhaustive binary32 tests, and randomized binary64 tests; it is the normative implementation source used for terminology here.
