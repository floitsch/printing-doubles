Before we start

The job, and Grisu’s bet

To print a double, find the shortest decimal that reads back as that double. Dragon4 does this exactly with big integers. Grisu uses only 64-bit integers, and pays for that with a small error that it has to track.

Remember: the rounding interval

A double is v = f · 2e. Halfway to its lower and upper neighbours lie the boundaries m− and m+. Any decimal strictly between them reads back as v. When f is even, a decimal exactly on a boundary also reads back as v, because ties round to even.

At a power of two the lower neighbour is only half as far away as the upper one, so m− sits a quarter gap below v instead of half a gap.

Remember: DiyFp

Grisu’s number type is a “do-it-yourself floating point”: a pair (f, e) of a 64-bit unsigned integer and an exponent, meaning f · 2e. It has no hidden bit and no special values. To normalize it, shift f left until its top bit is set. A double’s 53-bit significand then has 11 zero bits below it, and those spare bits are what Grisu works with.

The page has three parts:

  1. Park the binary point. One multiplication by a stored power of ten turns printing into ordinary integer work. This part ends with Grisu1, which prints correct but long results.
  2. Grisu in 16 bits. The whole of Grisu3 runs on half-precision floats, where you can check each step by hand. A map then shows which of the 31,743 positive inputs it rejects.
  3. The 64-bit debugger. Step through the production code of double-conversion with live values.

Part 1 · Park the binary point

One multiplication puts the binary point where we want it

The exponent e of a double ranges from −1074 to 971. Printing a number like that is awkward. It would be easy if its binary point sat at a known place inside a 64-bit register. The bits left of the point form an integer, and division prints it. The bits right of the point form a fraction: multiply it by 10, and the bits that cross the point are the next decimal digit.

Grisu gets there by multiplying v by a power of ten 10k, where k is chosen so that the binary point of the product lands in a fixed window. It prints the product’s digits and subtracts k from the decimal exponent. The power of ten comes from a table of 87 pre-rounded DiyFps: 10−348, 10−340, …, 10340, every eighth power.

Why every eighth power is enough

Multiplying two DiyFps adds their exponents, and keeping the top 64 of the 128 product bits adds 64 more: t = w.e + c.e + 64. The product has 64 bits, so its integral part has 64 + t bits and its fraction has −t bits. double-conversion requires −60 ≤ t ≤ −32:

  • At most 32 integral bits, so the integral part fits a uint32_t and prints with cheap 32-bit division.
  • At most 60 fraction bits, so fraction × 10 < 264. Four bits of headroom are enough for the ×10 step.

The window holds 29 values of t. Neighbouring table entries differ by a factor of 108, which is 8·log210 ≈ 26.58 in binary exponent, so their t values are 26 or 27 apart. A window that is 28 wide cannot fit between two neighbouring teeth, so at least one entry always fits, and sometimes two do. The code does not search for it. It computes the index directly:

k = ⌈(−60 − (w.e + 64) + 63) · log102⌉
index = ⌊(348 + k − 1) / 8⌋ + 1

When two teeth fit, this formula picks the one with the smaller t, which leaves more fraction bits.

Part 1 · The price

One rounded multiply, less than one unit off

A table entry only approximates 10k. Its 64-bit significand is rounded to nearest, so it is off by at most half a unit in its last place. Only 104, 1012 and 1020 are exact. The product w × c needs 128 bits. DiyFp::Times keeps the upper 64 bits, rounding half up. diy-fp.h does this without a 128-bit type: it splits each factor into two 32-bit halves and adds four partial products.

Each error is below half a unit of the result, so the computed product is off by less than one unit. That uncertainty is the price of avoiding bignums, and the rest of the algorithm is about living with it. It is small: because of the 11 spare bits, a unit is between 1/2048 and 1/1024 of the gap between neighbouring doubles. For 0.3 the rounding interval is 1250 units wide.

Part 1 · The payoff

Digits from a parked point

Once the point sits between bit −t and bit −t − 1, printing is just integer work. The integral part is at most 32 bits and prints with division. The fraction is multiplied by 10: the bits that cross the point form the next digit, and a mask removes them.

Part 1 · Grisu1

Correct, but long

The first algorithm in the paper stops here. It prints the digits of the product and appends −k as the exponent. The variant above is the paper’s “digit-gen-mix” (Figure 6). It prints the integral part, then fraction digits until it has 18 digits. The paper used the window −59 … −32; double-conversion’s −60 works just as well.

The result always reads back as the right double. The product is off by less than one unit, and cutting after 18 digits costs less than 10−17 relative, both far smaller than the gap between doubles. But it is 18 digits long even for 0.3. The paper’s simplest variant prints 1.0 as 10000000000000000000e-19.

Shorter output needs the rounding interval. Grisu2 and Grisu3 also scale the boundaries m− and m+, and generate only as many digits as the interval requires. But after scaling, the boundaries are also off by up to one unit, so the question “is this decimal inside the interval?” can come out as “maybe”. Part 2 shows how Grisu3 handles that, on numbers small enough to check by hand.

Part 2 · Grisu in 16 bits

The whole of Grisu3 on a pocket calculator

Grisu3 works the same way on any binary float format, so it can run on a small one. Take half-precision floats (11-bit significands) and 16-bit DiyFps. Every number then has at most five digits. With 5 spare bits instead of 11, one unit of error is relatively larger, so rejections are common enough to study.

Remember: half precision

binary16 has 1 sign bit, 5 exponent bits and 10 fraction bits. A value is f · 2e with 1024 ≤ f ≤ 2047 and −24 ≤ e ≤ 5 for normal numbers, and f < 1024 with e = −24 for subnormals. The largest value is 65504.

Our simplification. The pocket version runs the code of fast-dtoa.cc unchanged (DigitGen, RoundWeed, BiggestPowerTen) with q-bit integers instead of 64-bit ones. We changed two constants to fit: the window for t is −(q−4) … −(q−8), which is −12 … −8 for q = 16, and the table holds every power of ten rounded to q bits. A 5-value window needs a tooth every 3.3 binary exponents. This window and cache are our choices, not the library’s. The window keeps the real code’s 4 bits of headroom for ×10, and allows an integral part of at most 8 bits.

Here is the algorithm. The card below runs it.

  1. Scale w, m− and m+ by the same cached power. Each result is off by less than 1 unit.
  2. Widen: too_low = scaled m− − 1 and too_high = scaled m+ + 1. The true interval certainly lies inside (too_low, too_high). Call its width unsafe.
  3. Cut digits from the top of too_high. After each digit, rest is the part of too_high not yet printed, so the printed prefix equals too_high − rest. Stop at the first digit where rest < unsafe: the prefix is then the coarsest decimal inside the widened interval, which makes it the shortest.
  4. Round: lower the last digit toward w while that brings it closer to w. Give up if w’s own ±1 unit error makes “closer” impossible to decide.
  5. Weed: accept only if the result is at least 2 units below too_high and at least 4 above too_low. Only then is it certainly inside the true interval. Otherwise give up.

When Grisu3 gives up, double-conversion calls an exact bignum algorithm instead.

Part 2 · Rejections

When the card says no

There are two ways to fail, and both happen at q = 16:

  • = 1036·2−15 fails the safe-zone test. The shortest candidate in the widened interval is 316e−4, and it lands 0.4 units above too_low. That is inside the widened interval but not inside the safe zone. Rejecting is right: the exact m− is 0.0316009521484375, which is larger than 0.0316, so 0.0316 reads back as the neighbour 1035·2−15. Accepting would have printed a wrong number. The correct answer is 3162e−5, and with 20-bit DiyFps Grisu3 finds and accepts it.
  • fails the closeness test. The round step walks 3154 → 3153, but then cannot tell whether 3152 is closer to w. The midpoint between the two candidates lies inside w’s ±1 unit uncertainty. The correct answer is 3152e−5.

Grisu3 never guesses. When a candidate could be wrong, it rejects it, whether the candidate would have been right or not.

Part 2 · Exhaustive

All 31,743 positive half-precision inputs at once

Half precision has few enough values to run Grisu3 on all of them. Each pixel below is one positive value, one row per binade, with small numbers at the top. Adding bits to the DiyFp shrinks the unit, and the rejections shrink with it.

The rejections do not go to zero. From q = 32 on, exactly 2008 values (6.33%) are still rejected, and more bits will never change that, because for these values the answer depends on an exact coincidence:

  • 1024 exact ties. 0.15625 has two equally close shortest candidates, 1562e−4 and 1563e−4. Measured with even the smallest error, “which is closer” cannot be decided.
  • 492 answers exactly on a closed boundary. For 4112, the answer 411e1 = 4110 is exactly m−. It is valid, because 4112’s significand is even. But Grisu3 can never prove that a point lies exactly on a boundary rather than just outside it.
  • 492 shorter decimals exactly on an open boundary. For 4108, 4110 is again a boundary, but the significand is odd, so 4110 does not read back as 4108. Grisu3 cannot rule it out, so it rejects.

These coincidences need v or a boundary to hit a short decimal exactly. With 11-bit significands that happens often. With 53-bit ones it is rare: for random doubles, 80-bit or 96-bit arithmetic still rejects about 0.1%, compared with about 0.5% at 64 bits.

Closing

Three Grisus, three promises

All three variants share the cached power and the rounded multiply. They differ in what they do with the one-unit uncertainty.

Grisu1Grisu2Grisu3
Intervalnot usedshrunk by 1 unit at each endwidened by 1 unit at each end, and the result checked against the shrunk one
DiyFp bits neededq ≥ p + 2q ≥ p + 3q ≥ p + 3
Promisereads backreads back; usually shortestshortest and closest, or “cannot prove it”

The Grisu2 column is the paper’s Figure 7, which has no rounding step, so it prints the top of its shrunk interval: 7e-324 is valid but not the closest. On 100,000 random doubles we measured Grisu2 shortest 99.9% of the time, but also closest only 63%. A rounding step, as in the paper’s “Grisu2b”, fixes most of that. For 1e23 the shortest answer lies exactly on the boundary, outside Grisu2’s shrunk interval, so Grisu2 prints 16 digits. Grisu3 rejects about 0.5% of random doubles. In our tests it never accepted a wrong output: we compared every accepted result with an exact reference, for tens of thousands of random doubles and for every half-precision value in the pocket version.

In double-conversion, the shortest mode of DoubleToAscii calls FastDtoa, which is Grisu3. When that returns false, it calls BignumDtoa. The fast path handles the vast majority of inputs. The rare rejections cost a bignum computation but never a wrong digit. Later algorithms in this series remove the fallback by proving that their fixed-width arithmetic is always precise enough.
