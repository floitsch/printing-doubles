Unrounded scaling: four zones, one window

Russ Cox's converters never round too early. Their one primitive returns an unrounded number: the integer part plus two bits, just enough to round any way later. Scale the two edges of a double's rounding interval that way, and the shortest decimal can be read straight off a ruler.

First make the unrounded number something you can hold: a ruler with four kinds of places per unit. Then reuse that vocabulary for the whole shortest printer: two rulers, three arrows, one three-way decision. A six-level game at the end checks that it stuck.

The return type

Four kinds of places on a ruler

Every binary-to-decimal conversion ends the same way: a scaled number such as 30000000000000004.44… has to become an integer. But which rounding? The shortest printer below needs three different ones on the same kind of value: a ceiling for one edge, a floor for the other, and round-to-nearest for the value itself.

Cox's answer is to not round yet. His scaling primitive returns an unrounded number that keeps just enough to decide any rounding later:

⟨x⟩ = ⌊4x⌋ | (4x ≠ ⌊4x⌋)

That is the integer part of x, then a ½ bit (is the fraction at least ½?), then a sticky bit (is anything else left over?). Cox prints it as n.0, n.0+, n.5 or n.5+.

Remember

Floating-point hardware does the same. An adder keeps a few bits beyond the result (guard, round, sticky) so that it can round correctly at the very end. The sticky bit is the OR of everything shifted out past it: once a 1 has gone through, it sticks.

Measured against the integers, a real number can be in only four kinds of places: exactly on an integer, strictly between it and the half, exactly on the half, or strictly past the half. Drag the pin (or focus it and use the arrow keys). It snaps onto the dots so you can land on them exactly.

Try 6.25. There is no quarter mark: 4 · 6.25 = 25 = 110|0|1, so the ¼ bit lands exactly where the sticky bit goes and 6.25 reads 6.0+, the same as 6.01 or 6.49. A unit has four zones (two points and two open segments), not four quarters. And n.5 means “the ½ bit is set”, not the decimal digit 5.

The nudge buttons add ±1 to the code, which moves exactly one zone. Cox uses it as “add or subtract a tiny amount”. On an open segment that changes nothing a floor or ceiling can see. On a point it does: ⟨7.0⟩ floors to 7, but nudged down it is 6.5+ and floors to 6. That is how the printer will exclude an interval edge that falls exactly on an integer.

Rounding

Any rounding, decided later

With the code u = ⟨x⟩, every usual rounding is one addition and one shift. Add 0, 1, 2 or 3, then drop the two extra bits:

Round half to even adds 1 plus the low bit of the integer part, (u + 1 + ((u>>2)&1)) >> 2. Pick it and look at the strip: 8 catches five zones (7.5 up to 8.5) while 7 catches only three. Both 7.5 and 8.5 go to 8.

Round now, and you can lose

Why not simply round right away? Cox's example: we want 15.4 ÷ 6 = 2.566…, rounded to an integer. Round first and the fraction is gone before the division needs it. Keep the unrounded form and the division carries it along. Unrounded division is (u/d) | (u&1) | (u mod d ≠ 0): the sticky bit stays set, and a remainder sets it.

A bonus

The caller picks the tie rule

A true tie is the code n.5: ½ bit on, sticky bit off. Only then do the rounding rules disagree about the nearest integer, and the caller decides which one to use. Here is ⟨x · 100⟩ for a few doubles, the step behind printing them with two decimals:

1.125 is exactly representable, so ⟨112.5⟩ is a real tie. C's printf("%.2f") and Go round half to even and print 1.12; JavaScript's toFixed takes the larger candidate and prints 1.13. 1.005 is different: the double closest to it is 1.00499999999999989…, so its code is ⟨100.0+⟩. That is not a tie at all, and every “nearest” rule gives 1.00. The famous (1.005).toFixed(2) === "1.00" is correct rounding of the stored value.

The primitive

Same four zones, bigger numbers

Cox calls the primitive unrounded scaling:

uscale(x, e, p) = ⟨x · 2e · 10p⟩

It takes an integer x (a double's mantissa, or a nearby value), scales it by a power of two and a power of ten, and returns the code, never a rounded integer. Printing, fixed-width printing and parsing are all a few lines around it. The integers just get long:

Shortest printing

The decimal window

Remember

A double f has neighbours below and above. Halfway to each is a midpoint; together they bound the rounding interval (Cox calls its width the footprint). Every decimal strictly inside reads back as f.

A decimal exactly on a midpoint is a tie, and reading it back picks the neighbour with the even mantissa. So the midpoints belong to f when its mantissa is even (drawn ●) and not when it is odd (drawn ○).

At a power of two the gap below is half as wide as the gap above, so the footprint is skewed: ¾ of the usual width.

Cox's Short(f) never scales f first. It scales the two edges and reads the answer off the integers between them:

  1. Pick p so that the footprint, multiplied by 10p, is between 1 and 10 units wide. Now the integers are the candidate decimals, each meaning k · 10−p.
  2. Scale both midpoints with uscale. Then dmin = ceil(⟨min⟩) and dmax = floor(⟨max⟩), each nudged inward first when the mantissa is odd. Every integer in dmin…dmax is a valid output.
  3. Decide. (A) If one of them ends in 0, use it and strip its zeros. (B) Else, if there is only one, use it. (C) Else scale f too and round it half to even.

Step through it below. The top ruler is binary (f, its neighbours, the midpoints); the bottom ruler has the decimal integers at scale 10−p. Both share one axis, so the uscale arrows go straight down. Pick a preset or type a number (0.1+0.2, 2/3, 2^89 work too).

Why it works

Between 1 and 10 units wide

The width of the window decides everything. At least 1 unit means some integer fits. Less than 10 means at most one multiple of 10 fits. The footprint of f = m · 2e is 2e (or ¾ · 2e at a power of two), so

p = −⌊log10 2e⌋   or   p = −⌊log10(¾ · 2e)⌋

computed with one multiply and shift: log10Pow2(x) = (x·78913) >> 18 and skewed(e) = (e·631305 − 261663) >> 21. Note that p sets the zoom, not the number of digits in the output.

Try the zoom dial on : no integer fits, so no decimal with 16 or fewer digits reads back as this double. At six fit, none ending in 0. At fifty-six fit and six of them end in 0, so “the one ending in 0” is no longer a unique answer.

Why do the three cases give the shortest output? The valid integers are consecutive and fewer than ten. If a multiple of 10 is among them, it is the only one, and once its trailing zeros are stripped no other candidate is shorter (0.3 is 30000000000000000 · 10−17 = 3 · 10−1). No even shorter decimal was missed either: every coarser ruler's ticks are multiples of 10 on this one. Without a multiple of 10, all candidates have the same length, so the best is the one nearest f: the correctly rounded ⟨f⟩. If only one candidate exists, there is nothing to choose.

One corner needs care. A window exactly 1 unit wide would miss every integer if both its edges were integers and excluded. But the width is exactly 1 only when e = p = 0, and then the edges are the half-integers m ± ½. is such a case: the window is ⟨9007199254740990.5⟩ to ⟨9007199254740991.5⟩, and exactly one integer, f itself, fits.

The edges are rounded with ceil and floor, not to nearest, so that dmin and dmax are themselves inside the interval. When the mantissa is odd the edges don't belong to f, so each is nudged inward first. On an inexact code (a “+”) the nudge changes nothing. On an exact one it moves the result by one.

Two traps

Scale the edges, and keep the sticky bit honest

289: the nearest decimal is not valid

An obvious alternative is to round f itself to 16, then 17 digits until the result reads back. Cox shows that this can miss the shortest answer. f is a power of two, so the gap below it is half as wide and the window reaches only a quarter step down. f scaled by 10−11 is 6189700196426901.37…, so the correctly rounded 16-digit value ends in …901. But …901 lies outside the window (drawn as a red ghost): it would read back as the double below. …902 is inside, also has 16 digits, and is the answer. Scaling the edges finds it; rounding the value cannot.

1e23: an edge exactly on the ruler

The double nearest 1023 is 99999999999999991611392. Its upper midpoint is exactly 1023, so ⟨max⟩ = ⟨10000000000000000.0⟩ with no “+”. The mantissa 5960464477539062 is even, so the edge belongs to f, no nudge happens, the integer 10000000000000000 is valid and ends in 0: the output is 1e23. Tick “pretend the mantissa is odd” and the edge is nudged out. The answer becomes 9999999999999999e7, which still reads back but is not what an even mantissa deserves. Here the sticky bit decides the output, and this edge is one of the rare calls where the fast uscale needs its second multiply.

Check it by hand: 5e-324

The smallest subnormal is 4.94…e-324, with neighbours 0 and 9.88…e-324. The midpoints are 2.47…e-324 and 7.41…e-324, so at p = 324 the window is 2.47 … 7.41 and the valid integers are 3 to 7. None ends in 0, so f is scaled too: 4.94 → ⟨4.5+⟩ → 5. Output: 5e-324.

Epilogue

Making uscale fast

This page computes every ⟨⟩ with exact big integers. The real uscale gets the same bits from one table of 128-bit powers of ten, rounded up: multiply x by the top 64 bits of the entry and look at the bits about to be shifted out. If any of them is 1, the small error of the table cannot reach the result, the sticky bit is 1, and one 64×64 multiply is all it took. Only when they are all 0 (as for 1e23's upper edge) does a second multiply settle the borrow and the sticky bit. Approach B takes that machine apart and runs the real code with every variable shown.

Cox presents unrounded scaling as a synthesis. The shortest printer is adapted from Giulietti's Schubfach, including the observation that with the right p at most one valid decimal ends in 0. trimZeros is adapted from Dragonbox. That 128-bit tables are enough for exact results was found earlier by Hanson, Hack, Nadhezin and Giulietti, and Adams (Ryū); Eisel and Lemire use them for parsing. The check of the discarded bits echoes Slishman (1990). The sticky bit itself goes back to Len Harding and the BCC Model 1 around 1970. New is the combination: one unrounded primitive, with the check done before the second multiply.

Sources: R. Cox, Floating-Point Printing and Parsing Can Be Simple And Fast and Fast Unrounded Scaling: Proof by Ivy; code: rsc/fpfmt (fpfmt.go at ec108cb).
