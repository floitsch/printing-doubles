Toothless: nothing lives in the gap

Toothless prints doubles exactly, without big integers, by using a stored fraction in place of the exact power ratio. The stored fraction is wrong. This page shows why that never matters: every decision is a side question, and no question is small enough to fit between the stored fraction and the truth.

Starve a toy version's cache until a tiny float prints wrong, catch the culprit fraction sitting in the gap between stand-in and truth, then show with one line of Farey arithmetic why the real 63-bit table leaves that gap empty for every question a double can ask.

Every decision

Every decision is a side question

To print a double, an algorithm compares decimal candidates with the double's rounding boundaries. The boundaries are powers of two and the candidates are powers of ten, so the comparison needs the ratio of a power of two to a power of ten. For 6.62607015e-34 that ratio is 1050/2166. Written exactly, that takes big integers.

Toothless never works out that ratio. It only asks yes/no questions of one kind:

y/x above or below α = 2e/10k?

It answers each question with a stored stand-in num/den for α, with num and den below 263. The stand-in is not α. The whole trick is that the difference never changes an answer.

Remember

A float v has a rounding interval: from the midpoint with its lower neighbour (m⁻) to the midpoint with its upper neighbour (m⁺). A decimal inside it reads back as v. The printer wants the shortest such decimal and, among those, the closest to v.

A decimal exactly on a midpoint rounds to the neighbour with the even significand. So the interval includes its ends when v's significand is even and excludes them when it is odd.

A toy float

To make the numbers checkable by hand, this page uses a toy format with 8-bit significands: v = f · 2e with f from 128 to 255. Take

v = 183 · 2−18 = 0.000698089599609375

Its upper neighbour is 184 · 2−18 = 0.000701904296875, and the midpoint between them is m⁺ = 367 · 2−19 = 0.0006999969482421875. The short decimal 0.0007 is very close to that midpoint. Which side of it is 0.0007 on?

Write both sides as integers. 0.0007 = 700 · 10−6 and m⁺ = 367 · 2−19, so

0.0007 ≤ m⁺  ⟺  700 · 219 ≤ 367 · 106  ⟺  α = 219/106 ≤ 367/700

Here α = 219/106 = 8192/15625 = 0.524288. Check by hand: 367 · 15625 = 5,734,375 and 700 · 8192 = 5,734,400. The first is smaller, so 367/700 lies below α. The answer is no: 0.0007 is above m⁺ and does not belong to 183 · 2−18.

In this page, x is always the decimal-side integer (700) and y the binary-side integer (367). The question asks whether the lattice point (x, y) lies above or below the ray y = α · x.

All the questions look like this

Toothless (the C reference is continued.c) scales the double's boundaries so they share the stand-in's exponent, then makes four kinds of decisions. Each one compares a decimal-side integer X with a binary-side integer Y times the stand-in:

StepDecimal side XBinary side YQuestion
Top digits R = ⌊m⁺ scaled⌋candidate nupper boundarydoes n fit below m⁺?
Stop test (shortest length)digit prefix Rlower boundaryhas the prefix reached m⁻?
Round down the last digit2R − 10j2 · significandis the next-lower candidate closer to v?
Tie check2R − 10j2 · significandexactly halfway?

For doubles, every X and Y is below 259. The stand-in only has to give the same answers as α to fractions that small. That is a much weaker demand than being close to α.

Starve the cache

The stand-in lies

Toothless keeps one stand-in per power of ten. The toy cache stores the best fraction whose denominator stays below 2B, where B is the budget in bits. The real table uses a budget of 63 bits. First, starve the toy: B = 8.

The best stand-in for α = 8192/15625 with a denominator below 256 is 54/103 = 0.5242718…, a little below α. Ask the same question against it: 367 · 103 = 37,801 and 700 · 54 = 37,800. Now 367/700 is above the stand-in, and the answer is yes, 0.0007 fits. That is wrong.

It breaks two outputs. Toy Toothless prints 183 · 2−18 as 0.0007, which reads back as 184 · 2−18. For 184 · 2−18 the same fraction is the question "has 0.0007 reached my lower boundary?". The wrong "no" makes 184 print 0.000702 instead of its correct shortest output 0.0007.

The culprit is the question fraction itself. 367/700 = 0.5242857… lies between the stand-in 54/103 and α. A fraction in that gap is the only kind whose answer can flip.

A bigger budget fixes it. At B = 9 the stand-in is 259/494, a little above α: 367 · 494 = 181,298 is less than 700 · 259 = 181,300, so the answer is "no" again, as it should be. B = 6 also gets it right, but only by luck. Its stand-in 32/61 sits above α, so 367/700 is not in its gap, but the gap still holds 43/82.

The main picture

The wedge between two rays

Draw every question as a lattice point (x, y). The truth is the ray y = α · x, and the stand-in is a second ray through the origin, tilted by a tiny angle. Between them is a thin wedge, the gap. A point outside the wedge is on the same side of both rays, so the stand-in gives the true answer. Only a point strictly inside the wedge gets a wrong answer.

The two rays are far too close to tell apart on a normal plot, so the figure is sheared: every point moves vertically by −α · x. The true ray becomes the horizontal axis, and the vertical position of a point is y − α · x, how far it sits above or below the truth. For each x only the nearest lattice point is shown (the others are at least ½ away). Crosses mark the questions that the toy floats between 2−11 and 2−8, the ones that use this entry, actually ask at the chosen budget.

Slide B and watch the first point inside the wedge, marked with a ring. It moves right as the budget grows: 54/103 at B = 5, 367/700 at B = 7 and 8, 313/597 at B = 9, 8505/16222 from B = 10 to 12 (widen the x range to see the later ones). Once the first intruder lies beyond the question horizon, no question this entry can be asked has a wrong answer. At B = 14 the denominator 15625 fits, so the toy stores α itself and there is no gap at all.

At B = 9 one of the crosses sits in the wedge. That is the float 143 · 2−18, whose rounding question 572/1091 gets a wrong answer. The toy prints 0.000545 for it instead of 0.000546.

One line of arithmetic

Why the gap is empty

Being clean from B = 10 to 12 was luck. The guarantee comes from how the stand-in is built. Toothless's generator takes it from the continued fraction of α. The Stern–Brocot walk below gives the same fractions with nothing but additions: start with the bracket 0/1 < α < 1/0, and repeatedly replace one end by the mediant (a + c)/(b + d), keeping α inside. Stop when the next mediant's denominator would reach 2B.

Every bracket a/b < α < c/d on the way has bc − ad = 1. That is true for 0/1 and 1/0, and replacing an end by the mediant keeps it. Then:

Lemma. If a/b < c/d with bc − ad = 1, every fraction x/y strictly between them has y ≥ b + d.

Proof. cy − dx ≥ 1 and bx − ay ≥ 1 are positive integers, so y = y(bc − ad) = b(cy − dx) + d(bx − ay) ≥ b + d.

The walk stops because b + d ≥ 2B. So nothing strictly inside the final bracket has a denominator below 2B. The stand-in is one end of that bracket and α lies inside it, so the gap between them is clean up to 2B. For the toy's k = 6 entry, B = 13 gives b + d = 8,461, beyond the horizon of 8,130. That is a guarantee, not luck.

A tag fills the one hole the lemma leaves: a question exactly on the stand-in. The table stores whether the stand-in is above α ("higher") or below it ("lower"), and continued.c moves the boundary products by one unit so that such a point gets α's answer. For example, with a lower stand-in it adds 1 to p⁻ = lower boundary × num. In the real table α is never a small fraction itself, because its reduced denominator is 5k ≥ 528 > 264. So a question can never land exactly on α either.

The toy lab

Starve the whole cache

Now run toy Toothless on every toy float: f from 128 to 255 (columns) and e from −30 to 20 (rows), 6,528 floats in all. Each result is checked against an exact shortest-then-closest reference. The slider sets the budget for every cache entry at once.

Wrong answers come in three kinds. Some outputs read back as a different float. Some are longer than necessary. Some are the right length but not the closest. There is one toy-only kind, drawn dark: the question lands exactly on α. That can happen only when α's denominator 5k is small, which is never the case for an inexact entry of the real table.

The budget where the errors stop (11) is below the guaranteed one (14). Random testing would find nothing wrong from 11 on. The real table is a long way from that edge, as the next section shows.

Real scale

63 bits of stand-in, 59 bits of question

The real table in fraction_values.h has 325 entries, for k = 0 … 324. Entries 0 to 27 are α itself, since 527 < 263, so they have no gap. The other 297 are stand-ins with 63-bit numerators and denominators. The page loads the table and checks each entry with BigInt arithmetic:

Questions. A double's significand has 53 bits, so its boundaries 2f ± 1 have 54 bits (a power of two uses 4f + 2, which is 254 + 2). The shift that aligns them with the stand-in's exponent is 0 to 3. The page runs the worst case of every exponent: the shifted boundaries and R stay below 258, and only the doubled rounding test needs 59 bits (it stays below 259). So no question has a denominator of 259 or more, and every intruder has at least 263. That leaves about 4 bits of headroom.

Negative exponents. For small numbers continued.c uses the stored fraction upside down, den/num as a stand-in for 1/α. Swapping numerator and denominator turns a bracket with bc − ad = 1 into another one, so the same argument holds. The bound that matters there is the intruders' numerators, and those are at least 262.06.

One-sided, not closest. The draft paper argues that no small fraction can be closer to α than the stand-in, because each entry is "the best approximation". That is stronger than what the table satisfies. For 114 entries, the other end of the bracket is closer to α. For 46 of them, that closer fraction even has a smaller denominator. At k = 31, for example, a fraction with a 62-bit denominator on the other side of α is closer. It doesn't matter. The argument needs only that nothing lies between the stand-in and α, and that is the bracket property every entry has.

Honest status

What is checked, and what is not

Toothless is unpublished. The sources are a C reference (continued.c), the table and its generator, and an unfinished 2017 draft by the site's author. Treat the algorithm as argued and heavily tested, not proven.

  • Checked on this page: the gap lemma (one line, above), and that all 297 inexact entries are ends of a 63-bit Farey bracket with every intruder at 263 or more. The toy results are exhaustive: all 6,528 toy floats at every budget.
  • Incomplete in the draft: the proof has TODOs, and the minimality lemma appears twice, one copy labelled "(gemini)". The correctness argument relies on "best approximation", which 46 entries don't satisfy in the draft's own sense (the one-sided version above is what holds). The draft's rule for which semiconvergents are best approximations (0 < c < ak/2) is the reverse of the standard result, which needs c > ak+1/2.
  • Only in code: the tie rules (with a "higher" entry, a rounding test that lands exactly on the stand-in steps down once more; an exact entry rounds half to even) and the power-of-two fix-up after rounding. No version of the proof covers them.
  • Evidence, not proof: independent ports of continued.c match shortest-closest output (Python repr, JavaScript Number.prototype.toString) on millions of doubles, including constructed near-boundary cases. Random tests are weak evidence here. A cache rebuilt with only 50-bit fractions also passes 100,000 random doubles, yet it prints some constructed hard cases wrong.

To follow one real double through continued.c with the real table, and for the full list of claims, see approach B.
