Approach A · compare with approach B

Leap, then chop

Ryū never writes out the long exact decimal of a double. One multiplication jumps straight to its leading digits, as three exact integers. Then it erases trailing digits until one more erasure would leave no valid answer.

One two-act widget carries the same three integers a, b, c from the leap (a change of units, from binary quarter-steps to a fine decimal unit) into the chop (delete the last column while a coarser decimal grid still has a tick inside the rounding interval). All numbers are computed live from a BigInt port of d2s.c. The presets cover every branch, and the sabotage switches show which edge case each rule is there for.

Two moves

Every double owns an interval: all the reals that read back as that double. Ryū prints the shortest decimal inside it, and among the shortest the one closest to the double.

It gets there in two moves, and this page gives each one an act:

  1. The leap. Count the lower end of the interval, the double itself and the upper end in a fine decimal unit 10e10, keeping only whole units. The results are three exact integers, a ≤ b ≤ c, with 17 to 19 digits. One table multiply and one shift per integer do this. The hundreds of digits of the exact value are never produced.
  2. The chop. Delete the last digit of all three integers together, as long as a coarser decimal grid still has a tick inside the interval. Then round, using the last digit deleted from b.

Unlike Dragon4 and Grisu, Ryū does not produce digits from left to right. It starts with too many digits and removes them from the right.

Double

Act 1 · The leap

Change the ruler

Remember

A double is an integer times a power of two: v = m · 2e, with m < 253. Its neighbours are m − 1 and m + 1 on the same grid. Where m is exactly 252 (a power of two), the neighbour below sits half as far away.

The interval ends at the two midpoints, m ± ½. Ryū multiplies by 4 (two guard bits) and counts in the unit 2e2, where e2 = e − 2. Then all three points are integers: 4m − 2, 4m, 4m + 2, or 4m − 1 for the lower end at a power of two. One guard bit would not be enough for that closer midpoint.

A reader that rounds ties to even maps a midpoint back to our double only when m is even. So the ends of the interval are included when m is even and excluded when m is odd. The code calls this acceptBounds.

So far everything is counted in a binary unit. The leap re-counts the same three points in a decimal unit 10e10 and keeps only whole units. That is a floor:

a = ⌊(lower end) / 10e10⌋   b = ⌊v / 10e10⌋   c = ⌊(upper end) / 10e10⌋

In the figure the tinted band is the interval. The upper ruler shows the binary points; the lower ruler shows the decimal ticks, labelled a, b and c where the floors land. Ticks inside the band are decimal numbers that would read back as our double.

Act 1 · Choosing e10

Which decimal unit?

The unit depends only on the binary exponent. Ryū takes the finest power of ten whose counts fit comfortably in a 64-bit integer, then goes one step finer:

e2 ≥ 0: q = ⌊e2 · log102⌋ − 1, e10 = q
e2 < 0: q = ⌊−e2 · log105⌋ − 1, e10 = e2 + q

(Both are clamped at q ≥ 0.) Here q is the number of decimal digits the leap removes. For 0.3 it is 38, which is why the exact value never has to be written out. Move the slider to see the trade-off:

  • Too fine (slider left): the three counts get longer. Two steps finer, a 19-digit count needs more than 64 bits, and every extra digit is one more chop.
  • Too coarse (slider right): the leap throws away the digit that decides the rounding. With the “−1” removed from q, gets zero chops, so no rounding digit is ever seen. The floor b is printed as is, ending in …686: it reads back correctly but is not the closest shortest decimal. Two steps coarser, the band holds no tick at all.

At Ryū’s choice every normal double starts with 17 to 19 digits below 262, and the band holds between 3 and about 400 ticks: plenty of room, and the digits below the answer are all still there.

Act 1 · The multiply

One multiply, one shift

The floors are exact, but Ryū does not compute them with big numbers. First look at what it avoids: the exact decimal value of the double.

Dividing by 10e10 splits into a power of 2 and a power of 5. Powers of 2 are shifts, so the only real work is the power of five:

  • Small numbers (e2 < 0) need a multiplication by 5i with i = −e2 − q. The table stores the top 125 bits of 5i (truncated). Try : its entry is 518 · 283, exact because 518 fits.
  • Large numbers (e2 ≥ 0) need a division by 5q. The table stores 125 bits of the reciprocal 2k/5q, rounded up. Try : a division by 55 = 3125 turns into a multiply and a shift.

In C each entry is two 64-bit words, and each of a, b and c costs one 64×128-bit multiply whose low bits are dropped by the shift. A truncated constant makes each product a hair too small, and a rounded-up one a hair too big; the floor comes out right only if no exact product lies closer to an integer than that error. The paper proves that the 125-bit entries are always accurate enough, for every double, so there is no bignum fallback (see below).

Act 1 · The flags

Did the floor lose anything?

A floor forgets whether it threw anything away. Later, Ryū needs to know that for two reasons: an included endpoint that lands exactly on a tick is itself a valid output, and an exact value followed by a 5 is a true tie. It asks with a cheap divisibility test instead of looking at the dropped digits:

  • e2 ≥ 0: x · 2e2 / 10q is an integer exactly when 5q divides x (the 2q is always covered by 2e2).
  • e2 < 0: x · 5i / 2q is an integer exactly when 2q divides x, that is, when x ends in q zero bits.

The code tests only what can matter. For e2 ≥ 0 at most one of the three integers can be a multiple of 5 (they are at most 4 apart), and the test runs only for q ≤ 21. For e2 < 0 the ends 4m ± 2 have a single zero bit, so they only matter when q ≤ 1. When the upper end is exact but excluded, Ryū lowers c by one, so that c is always the largest legal tick.

Act 2 · The chop

Erase the last column

Deleting the last digit of an integer is ⌊x / 10⌋. Doing it to a, b and c together is the same as switching to a grid ten times coarser. The band stays where it is; its ticks thin out.

So the loop asks one question: does the coarser grid still have a tick inside the band? In integers: is ⌊c/10⌋ > ⌊a/10⌋? If yes, a shorter answer exists, so chop. If no, stop: the current grid is the coarsest that still has an answer, and its length is the shortest possible.

Hand-check . The smallest double gives a, b, c = 24, 49, 74 at the unit 10−325. One chop gives 2, 4, 7: the coarser grid still had the ticks 30…70 inside. Chopping again would give 0, 0, 0: no tick left. The candidates at 10−324 are 3 … 7, and the erased 9 says round b = 4 up: 5e-324.

Most doubles need one to three chops. “Human” decimals like need seventeen, because the interval is wide enough for a one-digit answer. Note that the test is not “do a and c share leading digits”: for 0.3 they start as 2999… and 3000…, and chopping continues anyway.

The two lamps track the exactness from Act 1 through the chops. “a exact” stays on only while every digit erased from a is 0: then a is still exactly the lower end. “b’s tail is 0” means every digit erased from b before the last one was 0. The code calls them vmIsTrailingZeros and vrIsTrailingZeros.

When the lower end is included and a is exact, the band is closed on the left, and a tick exactly at a counts too. The code handles this with a second loop that keeps chopping while a ends in 0: in ruler terms, “the coarser grid has a tick in [a, c]”. needs it: its lower end is exactly 7·1022. One ordinary chop, then sixteen chops in the second loop.

Act 2 · Round

The round step

When the loop stops, the answer is b or b + 1 on the current grid. Three rules pick one:

  1. The last erased digit. 5 or more: the double is at least halfway to b + 1, so take b + 1. Otherwise keep b.
  2. Exact ties go to even. If that digit is exactly 5 and b’s tail lamp is on, the double is exactly halfway: keep b if it is even. See and .
  3. b must be inside the band. If b = a and a is not a legal output, b lies at or below the lower end: take b + 1 whatever the digit says. See , where the digit is 4.

b + 1 never jumps past c: the interval is symmetric, or narrower below, so a double at least halfway to b + 1 cannot have c = b.

Act 2 · Edge cases

Break a rule

Each rule above exists for real doubles. The switches above the chopper remove one rule at a time. Each button below loads a double and a switch; the round card then shows what goes wrong.

  • The upper end is exactly 1023 and m is even, so it is a legal output. Treated as excluded, c drops to 999…9 and the result is 9.999999999999999e22: it reads back, but it is not the shortest.
  • At a power of two the lower half-gap is half as wide. Pretending it is 4m − 2 lets the chopper reach 1.780059086805761e-307, which reads back as a different double.
  • The digit 4 says keep b, but b = a lies below the band: 7.120236347223044e-307 reads back as a different double.
  • An exact tie between …2 and …3. Round half up prints …624.3, which reads back but is not the closest-to-even answer that JavaScript prints.

Beyond the widget

Why no fallback, and the fast path

Two things this page takes on trust. First, the floors from the truncated table are always exact: for each exponent the paper computes how close any product can come to an integer (with a Euclid-like min/max search, not by testing), and shows that 124 bits beat the worst case; d2s.c stores 125. So Ryū never needs a bignum fallback, unlike Grisu. Approach B lets you shrink the table and watch it break. Second, the real loop is faster than the one shown: in the common case (both flags off, about 99.3% of doubles) d2s.c skips the flag bookkeeping and first tries to remove two digits at once with a division by 100. It removes the same digits and gives the same result. It also has a shortcut for integers in [1, 253), which it prints directly and strips of trailing zeros.
