Tejú Jaguá: exact floors on a decade ruler

Tejú Jaguá picks a ruler with ticks 10F apart, finds which tick each end of the rounding interval lies at or just past, and decides the output with a few integer comparisons. It can work this way because every floor it reads is exact, which its generator proved in advance.

Runtime first. The reader picks the ruler, sees where the table row comes from, and then steps through the real C kernel next to a number line that shows the same integers. The offline proof only appears in the last section, as a preview of approach B.

The idea

Two whole numbers decide almost everything

Tejú Jaguá (Cassio Neri, 2021–2025, named after the first monster of Guaraní mythology) converts a double to its shortest decimal. It belongs to the same family as Ryū and Schubfach: scale the rounding interval by a power of ten, then read the answer off the scaled ends. What sets it apart is how little it computes at run time. It works out two floors, a and b: the tick at or just below each end of the interval. They are exact, so no error margin is needed. From a and b it knows whether a shorter decimal fits; if one doesn't, a third floor, c₂, picks the closest one.

Remember

A positive double is x = m · 2e with an integer mantissa m < 253 (for normal doubles m ≥ 252, the hidden bit included).

Its rounding interval runs from the midpoint with the double below to the midpoint with the double above: from (2m − 1)·2e−1 to (2m + 1)·2e−1, so it is 2e wide. Every decimal inside reads back as x.

A decimal exactly on an end is a tie, and reading it back picks the neighbour with the even mantissa. So the ends belong to x when m is even (drawn solid) and not when m is odd (drawn dashed).

We want the shortest decimal inside. If several are equally short, we want the one closest to x.

The plan has three parts: choose a ruler, find where the interval's ends fall on it, and decide. The page follows that order, with real doubles and the real C code.

Step 1

Pick the ruler

Think of the decimals with a given number of digits as the ticks of a ruler spaced 10D apart. Tejú picks one ruler, and the choice is the heart of the method: take the largest power of ten that fits in the interval's width 2e,

F = ⌊e · log10 2⌋
10F ≤ 2e < 10F+1

Measured in ticks of 10F, the interval is between 1 and 10 ticks wide. This gives two guarantees:

  • At least one tick is inside. The interval is at least one tick wide, so it can't slip between two ticks.
  • At most one long tick is inside. Every tenth tick (a multiple of 10 on this ruler) is a tick of the coarser ruler 10F+1, a decimal one digit shorter. Long ticks are 10 apart, and the interval is narrower than 10.

Try other rulers below. The drag control is a what-if: Tejú never tries rulers, it computes F in one step. A finer ruler (smaller D) catches many long ticks, so a program would have to search among them. A coarser ruler can miss the interval completely.

The formula is computed with one multiply and one shift, no logarithm: 1292913987 / 232 = 0.30102999…, which is log10 2 to 9 digits. So F = (1292913987 · e) >> 32, exact for every exponent a double can have.

Powers of two are the one exception. For m = 252 the double below is only half as far away, the interval is ¾ · 2e wide, and it can be less than one tick wide. Try 2^-1011 above. Tejú keeps the same F and handles this case separately (see Ties and powers of two).

Step 2

One table row per decade

To use the ruler, Tejú needs the interval's ends in units of 10F, for example the upper end (2m + 1) · 2e−1 / 10F. The factor 2e−1/10F comes from a table, but the table has one row per decimal exponent F, not one per binary exponent e. F = ⌊e · log10 2⌋ stays flat for three or four consecutive exponents, a staircase:

Within one stair, the exponents differ only by r = e − e0 ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3}, where e0 is the first exponent of the stair. Tejú rewrites the value so that it uses the stair's first exponent:

m · 2e = (m ≪ r) · 2e0

The leftover power of two becomes a left shift of the small operand, and a 53-bit mantissa shifted by up to 3 (and doubled, plus one) still fits in 64 bits. The table row is the 128-bit number

M = ⌊2128 · 2e0−1 / 10F⌋ + 1

and scaling is mshift(n, M) = ⌊n · M / 2128⌋. The shift is always 128, so the result is just the top 64-bit word of a 64 × 128-bit product, and no shift instruction is needed. The readout above multiplies a sample mantissa for the chosen e, and the top word is exactly the floor of the scaled upper end.

The residual r also costs only one multiply: the low 32 bits of 1292913987 · e are the fractional part of e · log10 2, the distance up the current stair. Dividing it by 1292913987 (one stair step) counts the steps: r = (uint32_t)(1292913987 · e) / 1292913987.

Step 3

Step through the kernel

Now the whole runtime. Pick a double and step through teju_to_decimal. The number line is in units of 10F: its ticks are integers. The code beside it is the real kernel from teju.h, with the current lines highlighted and each variable's value written next to it.

The number line shows the exact fractional parts of the ends for you. Tejú never computes them. It only has the floors a and b, and the generator guarantees they are right.

For 0.1, the ruler is 10−17. The interval runs from 9999999999999999.861… to 10000000000000001.249… ticks, so a = 9999999999999999 and b = 10000000000000001. The only possible long tick is s = 10 · ⌊b/10⌋, the multiple of 10 at or below b. Here s = 1016, which is greater than a, so it lies inside. Stripping its zeros gives 1 · 10−1.

Why is s the only candidate? Any other multiple of 10 is either above b, and so past the upper end, or at most s − 10. The interval is less than 10 ticks wide, so s − 10 lies below the lower end. If s misses, no shorter decimal exists, and Tejú returns the tick of 10F closest to x. The interval is at least one tick wide, so that tick is inside. It computes one more exact floor, c₂ = ⌊2x / 10F⌋. With c = ⌊c₂/2⌋, x lies between ticks c and c + 1, and the parity of c₂ says which half: even means x is in the left half, odd means the right half.

The fine print

Ties and powers of two

Because a and b are floors, “s > a” is exactly the right test in almost every case. Two situations need more care, and each has a preset:

  • An end is exactly a tick. The double nearest to 1023 is 99999999999999991611392, and its upper end is exactly 1023: 2m + 1 = 11920928955078125 = 523. On the ruler 107 that makes b = s = 1016. The floor cannot tell “just past s” from “exactly s”. The code checks exactness separately: the end is a tick exactly when 5F divides mb, is_tie(f, m_b). It is, and m is even, so the end belongs to x and the output is 1e23. The mirror case s == a (the lower end is exactly s) is accepted only when it is a tie and m is even. The aside below shows how the divisibility test works without dividing.
  • A midpoint between two ticks. On the closest path, c₂ odd usually means “round up”. If 2x/10F is exactly the odd integer c₂, x sits exactly halfway, and the even one of c and c + 1 wins. Tejú tests that with the same trick, is_tie(-f, c_2), which can only happen for −26 ≤ F ≤ 0.
  • Uncentred: m = 252. The lower end is only 2e−2 below x, so to_decimal_uncentred computes a from ma = (4m − 1) ≪ r and halves the floor. The rest is the same, with one extra exit: the lower half of the interval can be narrower than half a tick, so the tick c below x can be outside. When c == a (and the lower end isn't exactly a tick), the answer is c + 1. 2^56 takes that exit.
  • No tick at all. An uncentred interval is only ¾ · 2e wide and can fall between two ticks, so a == b. Unless the lower end is exactly the tick a, Tejú moves one decade finer. On the ruler 10F−1 the interval is at least 7.5 ticks wide, and c₂ = mshift(40m ≪ r) gives the closest tick there. For binary64 this happens for 33 powers of two. 2−1011 prints as 4.5569512622227484e-305.
  • Small integers (x = m · 2e with −52 ≤ e ≤ 0 and x a whole number) skip the multiply entirely. x is m ≫ −e, and only the trailing zeros are removed.

Aside

Divisibility without dividing

Tejú asks “is n a multiple of 5f?” for ties and “is n a multiple of 10?” while stripping zeros. Neither question uses a division. It multiplies by a modular inverse and compares. Here is the trick on an 8-bit clock with 256 positions (the real code uses 64 bits).

205 is the inverse of 5 modulo 256: 5 · 205 = 1025 = 4 · 256 + 1. Multiplying by 205 (mod 256) shuffles the clock, but it sends every multiple 5k to exactly k. So all 52 multiples of 5 land on 0 … 51, and everything else lands above. After one more rotate right by one bit, even quotients are halved and odd ones get their low bit moved to the top, which makes them ≥ 128. So the multiples of 10 are exactly the results below 26, and each result is n / 10.

At 64 bits the same works for every power of five. The generator emits a table minverse[f] = (5−f mod 264, ⌊264/5f⌋) with 27 rows. It stops there because 527 exceeds every number the kernel can test (at most 320 · (253 − 1)). For F < 0 or F > 26 an exact tie is impossible, and the test is skipped.

The 27 rows of minverse and the 1e23 check

remove_trailing_zeros uses the f = 1 row with the rotate: q = ror(m · 0xcccccccccccccccd). If q < ⌊(264 − 1)/10⌋ + 1, then 10 divides m and q is already m/10. Each digit costs one multiply, one rotate and one compare. The kernel above lists every iteration when the result is stripped.

Epilogue

Why may the kernel trust a and b?

Everything above assumed that mshift returns the exact floor. That is not automatic. M = ⌊2128 · α/δ⌋ + 1 (with α/δ = 2e0−1/10F in lowest terms) is slightly too large, so n · M / 2128 overshoots the true n · α/δ by a small amount that grows with n. The floor comes out wrong exactly when the true value lies just below an integer, closer to it than the overshoot.

Tejú's generator, a C++ program using Boost.Multiprecision, rules that out before any code ships. For each of the 617 rows it finds the worst n among all operands the runtime can pass: every mantissa, shifted and doubled, plus the extra power-of-two operands. That is about 258 values, and it doesn't try them one by one; a Euclid-like recursion finds the worst one in at most a few dozen steps. It then proves that even that n gets the right floor. If any row failed, it would refuse to emit the table.

Across all rows, the tightest safety margin is 3.71 bits (F = −199) and the median is about 14 bits. So 128 bits are enough, with room to spare, and the kernel never needs a correction step. Approach B starts from this proof and lets you break the multiplier.
