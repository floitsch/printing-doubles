Binade meets decade

Dragon4 finds the decimal exponent by multiplying by ten, one decade at a time: hundreds of bignum steps for the extreme doubles. David Gay's dtoa (1990) and Burger & Dybvig (1996) read it off the binary exponent instead. The guess is off by at most one, and one exact comparison fixes it.

Two stacked rulers, binades above and decades below, make the key speedup visible. A binade is 0.301 decades wide, so it holds at most one power of ten, so the exponent bits leave only two candidates. What dtoa adds on top is shown afterwards as a ledger of 2s and 5s.

1 · Where Dragon4 spends its time

The digits were never the slow part

Remember: Dragon4's digit loop

Steele & White's Dragon4 works with four exact integers. r/s is the value v, scaled so that it lies in [0.1, 1). m⁺/s and m⁻/s are the half-gaps to the neighbouring doubles, scaled the same way.

Each round multiplies r, m⁺ and m⁻ by 10, takes the digit d = ⌊r/s⌋ and keeps r mod s. It stops as soon as the digits so far already lie inside the rounding interval (r < m⁻: emit d) or rounding the last digit up lands inside it (r + m⁺ > s: emit d+1).

A double never needs more than 17 digits, so that loop runs at most 17 times. The expensive part comes before it: getting r/s into [0.1, 1). Dragon4's scaling loop does that one decade at a time. It multiplies s by 10 (or r, m⁺ and m⁻ by 10) and compares again, once for every power of ten between 1 and v. Pick a number and race the two strategies.

The treadmill's cost is V-shaped in the decimal exponent, and the integers grow on every step. Burger & Dybvig timed it on 250,680 doubles: iterative scaling took 70 times the CPU time of their estimate (Table 2 of the paper). Both papers get rid of the treadmill with the same observation. The rest of this page explains it.

2 · The core idea

Your exponent already knows the decade

Remember: what the exponent field says

A double stores v = f · 2e with an integer significand f. The exponent field tells you the binade, the power of two with 2s ≤ v < 2s+1. Here s = e + len(f) − 1, where len(f) is f's length in bits. For normal doubles, s is just the unbiased exponent.

Convention on this page Results are written d.ddd × 10E (scientific, as in 1e23). Burger & Dybvig write 0.d₁d₂… × 10k, so their k is E + 1. dtoa's internal k is E, and its returned decpt is E + 1.

On a logarithmic axis every decade is 1 unit wide, and every binade is log₁₀ 2 ≈ 0.30103 units wide. Because 0.301 < 1, a binade contains at most one power of ten. So once you know the binade, only two candidates for E are left: the decade the binade starts in, or the next one. Drag v and watch the blue binade project onto the decimal ruler.

The bits of the exponent cannot tell 700 from 1000: both live in binade 2⁹ = [512, 1024), whose single power of ten is 1000. The candidates are E = 2 and E = 3. Only the rest of v decides between them, so the estimate needs one more piece of information, and Section 4 shows how to get it with one comparison.

Which end decides? Burger & Dybvig choose the exponent from the upper end of the rounding interval, high = v + ½ ulp, not from v. Their output then never has to carry into a new leading digit. For most numbers this makes no difference. It does for the double nearest 10²³, which is 99999999999999991611392. Its high end is exactly 10²³, and since the significand is even that end belongs to the interval. So B&D's target is E = 23, while ⌊log₁₀ v⌋ = 22 is dtoa's target. The ruler's truth line follows whichever algorithm the selected estimator belongs to.

3 · The estimators

Two ways to guess: err low or err high

Burger & Dybvig: the binade's left edge

Their fast estimator (Figure 3 of the paper) uses nothing but s:

k = ⌈s · log₁₀2 − 10⁻¹⁰⌉  ⇒  Ê = k − 1

That is two floating-point operations. It is the decade in which the binade starts, so it is never too high. It is one too low exactly when high has passed the power of ten inside the binade: in 2⁹ that is [1000, 1024). Over all normal doubles (every bit pattern counted once) that happens for 16.8%. The −10⁻¹⁰ protects against the rounded product landing just above an integer. Its only side effect is that binade [1, 2) always gets the low guess.

Gay: a tangent line through the significand

dtoa also looks at the significand. With v = x · 2i and 1 ≤ x < 2, log₁₀ v = i · log₁₀2 + log₁₀ x. Gay replaces log₁₀ x by its tangent at x = 1.5:

ds = (x − 1.5)·0.289529654602168 + 0.1760912590558 + i·0.301029995663981,   Ê = ⌊ds⌋

log₁₀ is concave, so the tangent lies above the curve everywhere: by 0.0313 at x = 1 and by 0.0198 at x = 2. On top of that, the constant is rounded up by 10⁻¹³ to absorb the rounding error of i · 0.30103 (dtoa's own comment gives the reason). Gay's guess is therefore never low, and at most one high. Because it uses the significand, its miss zone in 2⁹ shrinks to [970.73, 1000), just below the power of ten, where B&D's guess is right. Over all normal doubles it misses 0.83%. In 41 binades the miss zone sits at the very top of a binade that holds no power of ten, because the power lies just above the binade's upper edge.

4 · The repair

One comparison repairs the guess

Burger & Dybvig. They take 10|est| from a table (10⁰ … 10³²⁵). They multiply it into s when est ≥ 0, and into r, m⁺ and m⁻ otherwise. Now (r + m⁺)/s = high / 10est, so "did high reach 10est?" is the single comparison r + m⁺ ≥ s. It is > when high is excluded, because f is odd.

The repair is free because of where the ×10 lives. The digit loop's first act would be to multiply r, m⁺ and m⁻ by 10, and B&D move that multiplication to the call site. If the guess was right, they do it; it was needed anyway. If the guess was one low, fixing the scaling would take one more ×10. That is exactly the ×10 the loop is about to do, so both are skipped. A low guess is actually one multiplication cheaper.

Gay. dtoa's guess is never low, so the repair can only lower it. When 0 ≤ k̂ ≤ 22 it compares in doubles, v < 1ek̂, which is exact because every 10k up to 10²² is a double. Otherwise it sets k_check. After building the bignums b and S, with b/S = v/10k̂, it tests b < S. If that holds, k drops by one, and b and the margin mhi are multiplied by 10.

Why floating-point logs alone are not enough.

5 · Measured

How often does the guess miss?

Draw random normal doubles (random exponent field, random significand bits), compute each estimator, and subtract the exact target. Every bar is either on the documented side or at zero.

B&D's floating-point log10 estimator (their Figure 2) almost never misses on random input. It still misses where it matters: exact powers of ten such as 1000 and 10¹⁰⁰, where log₁₀ is exactly an integer, and the double nearest 10²³. So B&D chose the crudest estimator on purpose, because its misses cost nothing. Gay's estimator is better but needs a real repair, and that repair costs a bignum comparison plus a ×10 when k̂ is outside [0, 22].

6 · What dtoa adds

A ledger of 2s and 5s

The estimate removes the treadmill. Gay's dtoa then makes the integers of the digit loop smaller. It does this with bookkeeping: before it builds any bignum, it writes each of its three integers as odd × 2x × 5y and does arithmetic on the exponents only. The key identity is 10k = 2k · 5k. The 5s of 10k all go to one side, so they never need cancelling. The 2s cancel by subtracting counters. Step through it with dtoa's own variable names.

The payoff: smaller integers

That is an honest saving of about 30%, not an order of magnitude. The alignment shift (dshift) adds a few bits back on purpose: it gives S's top 32-bit word exactly four leading zero bits. That is what makes dtoa's digit extraction, quorem, an "estimate, then fix once" step, just like the exponent. It guesses q̂ = ⌊top word of b / (top word of S + 1)⌋, which never overshoots, subtracts q̂·S, and adds one more if the remainder is still ≥ S.

The fast lane: small integers skip the ledger

If v is an integer (after d2b, the binary exponent is ≥ 0) and k ≤ 14 (Int_max, so v < 10¹⁵), dtoa never touches a bignum. It divides by 10k in doubles, subtracts, multiplies by 10, and stops when the remainder is exactly zero. Below 2⁵³ every one of those operations is exact, and such an integer's own digits are its shortest representation.

7 · If you open dtoa.c today

The same idea, one level faster

Everything above is dtoa's classic path: Gay's 1990 paper, and the netlib file before 2016. Since the 20160429 netlib release, dtoa.c defaults to a faster route in mode 0 on machines with 64-bit integers. Only -DNO_BF96 or -DNO_LONG_LONG turns it off. The route has four stages:

  1. Exact k. A hint table indexed by the binary exponent, plus one comparison against a stored power of ten. It is the two-rulers idea again, now without the tangent line.
  2. Exact 64-bit integer digits when the scaled numbers fit.
  3. A 96-bit approximation of 10−k with error tracking. It produces the digits when the error bound can decide them.
  4. Fallback (Fast_failed1): the bignum ledger and digit loop from this page.

In that default build, the small-integer lane and the k_check repair are compiled out. One more thing that often confuses readers: mode 0 (shortest output) never uses dtoa's older floating-point try_quick path. That path serves modes 2–5, which ask for a fixed number of digits (ilim ≥ 0), and mode 0 sets ilim = −1.

In one breath. Dragon4 searched for the decade. A binade is 0.301 decades wide, so the exponent bits narrow it to two candidates. B&D guess low and make the fix free by reusing the digit loop's ×10. Gay guesses high with a tangent line and fixes with a double compare (0 ≤ k̂ ≤ 22) or one bignum compare. dtoa then shrinks the integers with a 2/5 ledger and skips bignums entirely for small integers. One last difference: when the last digit is an exact tie, B&D's paper rounds up and dtoa picks the even digit. Both outputs are shortest and round-trip.
