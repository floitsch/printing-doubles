Errol: the lab notebook

Errol prints doubles with about 106 bits of floating-point arithmetic and no run-time check. That design came out of experiments. Rerun them here, in your browser. Then run the search that finds the few inputs where the fast arithmetic is fooled, and rebuild Errol's exception table.

Tell Errol as a series of measurements. Errol1's failures pile up in one narrow range. Every one of them is an exact midpoint, and exact integers fix those. Without the safety margin, random testing finds nothing. Then comes the hunt for the needles: a Euclid-style search that typically visits a few dozen midpoints out of 252 per binade, and reproduces the real 432-entry table.

Setup

What Errol computes

Errol is a boundary algorithm, like Steele & White's. It never searches decimals one by one. It computes the two edges of the rounding interval, scales both into [1, 10), and peels off decimal digits from both at once. While the two edges agree on a digit, every number in between shares that digit, so it is printed. At the first digit where they differ, one more digit is enough. Errol picks it by rounding the middle of the interval.

Remember

A double v has two neighbours. The midpoints m− and m+ between v and its neighbours bound the rounding interval: every decimal inside reads back as v. The midpoints themselves belong to v only if v's significand is even (ties go to even).

The paper uses three words. Correct: the output lies in the interval, so it round-trips. Optimal: no decimal with fewer digits lies in the interval (shortest). Best (added in the code later): the closest among the shortest.

Errol's twist is the arithmetic. It computes the edges in double-double: a value is a pair val + off of two doubles, about 106 bits in total. The edges are therefore almost exact. The rest of this notebook asks what happens in the rare cases where "almost" is not enough.

Notebook · entry 1

Errol1: safe, but sometimes too long

The double-double arithmetic has an error. The paper derives a bound by hand (its Theorem 4): the computed edges are off by at most 79 · 2−106 relative to v. Errol1 plays it safe. It shrinks the interval by more than that error: the half-gap is divided by 2 + ε instead of 2, with ε = 8.77·10−15 (79 · 2−53). The shrunk interval is guaranteed to lie inside the true one, so every output is correct.

The price: a short decimal in the thin shaved-off strip is lost, and Errol1 then prints a longer one. How often does that happen, and where? The experiment: draw random doubles from every one of the 2,046 binades [2e, 2e+1), print them with Errol1, and compare the length with the shortest output (JavaScript's toExponential(), whose digit count the language spec requires to be minimal).

The result is lopsided. Errol1 is shortest about 99.97% of the time (the paper reports 99.973% on a billion inputs). The failures, however, sit only between 254 and about 270, where they reach a fifth of all inputs. Everywhere else the sample shows none. Errol1's own warning flag (it also runs a widened interval and flags disagreement) fires on every real failure, and on about as many false alarms.

Notebook · entry 2

Every failure is a midpoint

Look at the failures one by one. In [254, 255) the doubles are the multiples of 4, so the midpoints are v − 2 and v + 2. Pick a binade:

No failure is a near miss. In every case the shortest decimal sits exactly on the edge of a closed interval (v's significand is even). Shrinking the interval by any amount, however small, loses it. So more precision would not help: Errol1 could use a thousand bits and still fail here. The paper turns this into a theorem.

Theorem 6 (pathological midpoints). If a midpoint is shorter than every decimal strictly inside the interval, then that midpoint is an integer, and it lies in [254, 2131].

Why only there? (three short arguments)
  • Not a fraction. A non-integer midpoint ends in binary …1 after the point, so in decimal it ends in …5 far to the right. The paper shows it has at least 17 significant digits, never fewer than the 17-digit decimals the interval always contains. Example: the midpoint above 0.1 is 0.100000000000000012490009027033061087131500244140625.
  • Not too big. An integer midpoint is an odd number below 255 times a power of two. Trailing decimal zeros need factors of five, and an odd number below 255 holds at most 23 of them (523 < 255 < 524). A midpoint of about 40 digits therefore keeps at least 17 significant ones. The paper puts the cut-off at 2131.
  • Not too small. In [253, 254) the doubles are the even integers and the midpoints the odd ones. An odd integer has no trailing zero, so a midpoint is never shorter than the double next to it.

Errol2 acts on the theorem. Inputs between 253 and 3.4·1038 (just below 2128) no longer use double-double. Their midpoints are integers that fit in 128 bits, so Errol2 computes them exactly and needs no narrowing. The same experiment, with Errol2:

The spike is gone. Outside the integer range, Errol2 still narrows. That leaves a theoretical chance of losing a short decimal, but in a billion random inputs the paper saw none.

Notebook · entry 3

Drop the safety margin

Narrowing costs time: Errol1 and Errol2 track four edges instead of two. Narrowing also only protects against something random testing can no longer see. So Errol3 drops it and uses the double-double midpoints as they are. The error can now push a computed edge past a short decimal that lies extremely close to the true edge. Errol3 can then print a decimal just outside the interval (wrong), or miss a short one just inside (too long). Here is Errol3's fast path without its table, as the code calls it (errol3u). Integers are handled as in Errol2, and [16, 253] goes to an exact fixed-point routine.

Random testing finds nothing, but that is not proof. With 263 positive doubles, a few hundred bad inputs would be invisible to any random test. The 342 hand-picked doubles show that they exist. Errol3's answer is to find every such input ahead of time, print it slowly and exactly once, and store the result in a table. That table, enum3.h, is checked before the fast path runs. The rest of the page is about how to find them.

The hunt

From "near a short decimal" to "near zero on a clock"

Brute force is out of the question: the double-double path covers 1,975 binades with 252 doubles each. The search only needs candidates, though: midpoints with a short decimal so close that the arithmetic error could matter. For binades above 2128, the midpoints are integers, and the condition becomes modular arithmetic.

  1. The midpoints form a progression. In [2e, 2e+1) they are mk = 2e + (2k + 1) · 2e−53 for k = 0 … 252 − 1.
  2. Short means many trailing zeros. A decimal only matters if it is shorter than 17 digits, because 17 digits always fit in the interval. Numbers in this binade have about 0.301·(e + 1) digits, so a decimal with at most 16 significant digits is a multiple of 10n, with n = ⌊(e + 1) log10 2⌋ − 15.
  3. Split 10n = 2n · 5n. Every mk is a multiple of 2n already, so divide it out: Mk = mk / 2n = M0 + k·α with α = 2e−52−n. The short decimals become the multiples of τ = 5n.
  4. Near means a small residue. Midpoint k is a suspect only if Mk mod τ lies within ±Δ of 0. The budget Δ covers the error bound with room to spare. In ulps it is 179 · 2−52 ≈ 4·10−14 of the gap between doubles (the code's constant for large binades).
find every k < 252 with |(M0 + k·α) mod τ| ≤ Δ

Picture a clock with τ ticks. The midpoints step around it, α ticks at a time, and the short decimals all sit at 12 o'clock. We want every step that lands within Δ of 12.

The mirror case: binades below 25 (non-integer midpoints)

Below 253 the midpoints have binary fractions. The code multiplies them by 10−e+53, which makes them integers without changing their digits. Now the factor that comes for free is 5n instead of 2n, and the condition becomes "residue mod 2n near 0". It is the same clock with τ = 2n, and the same search, with budget 79 · 2−52 ulp. (In test/main.c α, τ and M0 also carry a common factor 251.) The search covers binades 2−1074 … 24 and 2128 … 21023: exactly where Errol3 uses double-double. Everything in between goes to exact code.

The hunt · by hand

A clock you can check with a pencil

Shrink everything. Take a toy format with 5 significant bits in [1024, 2048): the values are 1024 + 64j, the midpoints 1056 + 64k (k = 0 … 15). Call a decimal "short" if it has two significant digits: a multiple of 100. The error budget is ±4. Every midpoint and every multiple of 100 is divisible by 4, so divide by 4. The midpoints become 264 + 16k, the multiples of 100 become multiples of 25, and the budget becomes ±1. That is a 25-tick clock with step α = 16.

Two ideas make this fast. First, the records: the steps k·α whose residue gets closer to 0 than any shorter step, kept in one list for each side. Each new record is the newest record on one side plus an earlier one from the other side. That is the bookkeeping of Euclid's algorithm, or of the continued fraction of α/τ. The number of records depends on that continued fraction, not on 252: across all real binades the median is under 200. Second, the descent: from the current residue, add the largest record of the opposite sign that does not overshoot by more than we are away. The residue ping-pongs across 0 and shrinks every hop. Once one hit is found, adding each record that stays within the budget reaches all the others (the paper's Theorems 9 and 10).

The hunt · real scale

One real binade, 252 midpoints, a few dozen hops

Now the real thing. For [2227, 2228) the clock has τ = 553 ≈ 1.1·1037 ticks, the step is α = 2122, and the budget is Δ = 179 · 270 ≈ 2.1·1023 ticks. The chart shows each hop of the descent, measured in ulps: how far the midpoint is from the nearest multiple of 10n, as a fraction of the gap between doubles.

A few dozen hops replace 4.5·1015 checks. On this binade, 340 of the 342 suspects print fine; the budget is generous. Two do not: one is a correct but 17-digit output where 16 digits fit, and the other is a 16-digit output that reads back as a different double. Both bit patterns appear in enum3.h.

The hunt · everything

Rebuild the whole table

Run the search on all 1,975 binades and test every suspect. This is what test/main.c does with --enum3. On a laptop it takes roughly 10–30 seconds, and it runs in a background worker.

Every failing input the search finds is in the table, and every table entry is found. That checks the pipeline: the search, the test and the table agree. It does not check that the budget Δ is large enough. If the hand-derived error bound were wrong, a real failure could lie outside every suspect's budget, and this rebuild would agree with the table just the same.

The ledger

What was claimed, and what it rests on

Claimed
Errol3 is correct (round-trips) and optimal (shortest) for every double. Closest was never guaranteed. The March 2016 commit that added "best" selection says Errol "provides a best effort to choose the best output but does not guarantee a correct selection".
Rests on
The error bound 79 · 2−106 (paper, Theorem 4), which sets Δ, and the hand proofs of the necessary conditions (Theorems 7–8) and of the search (Theorems 9–10). None of this is machine-checked. Rebuilding the table above verifies the pipeline, not the soundness of Δ. (This page's tests compare the search with brute force on 3,000 small random clocks and find the same hits every time. That supports Theorems 9–10 but says nothing about Δ.)
Tied to
The exact sequence of floating-point operations in errol.c: round-to-nearest doubles, no fused multiply-add, no extended precision. A port that rounds differently even once needs its own table. (This page's ports of errol1, errol2 and errol3u match the C bit for bit on 212,000 test inputs.)
Table size
The paper's introduction says 141 inputs, and §6.4 says 45. The repository regenerated the table in 2016 together with the best-selection change. The current enum3.h has 432 entries: 286 outputs that do not round-trip, 145 that are too long, and DBL_MAX.
Not searched
The exact paths: errol_fixed for [16, 253] and errol_int for (253, 3.4·1038). The reference errol_int has bugs that random testing does not reach: Pick an input to run the port of errol_int. When the answer has one digit, the output is empty and one byte is written before the buffer (GitHub issue #12). A rounded-up 9 becomes : without a carry. The two gaps are also swapped (the low edge uses the upper gap). That matters only at powers of two, where the gaps differ: 264 prints a decimal that reads back as a different double.
Speed
The first version of the paper reported Errol3 about 2× faster than Grisu3. The benchmark had built Grisu3 without optimizations. The README correction (the error was spotted by Grisu3's author) says about 2× slower, and the updated paper says 2.4× slower than Grisu3 with a bignum fallback, and 5.2× faster than Dragon4.

None of these bugs is a flaw in the idea. The idea is to replace a run-time check with an offline search over a clock, backed by exact arithmetic where coincidences are provably exact. It is sound in outline, and it is the one part of Errol that no other algorithm in this series shares. For the zoom onto a single boundary and the double-double digit loop, read approach A.

Sources: Andrysco, Jhala, Lerner, “Printing Floating-Point Numbers: A Faster, Always Correct Method”, POPL 2016 (corrected PDF); reference code github.com/marcandrysco/Errol at 5364de4 (lib/errol.c, lib/enum3.h, test/proof.c, test/main.c); issue #12.
