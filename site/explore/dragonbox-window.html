One number, not three

A shortest-output printer has one job: find the shortest decimal inside the double's rounding interval. If several have that length, it picks the one closest to the double. Schubfach, Dragonbox's direct ancestor, computes three numbers for this: the lower end x, the centre y and the upper end z.

Dragonbox computes only one: the upper end z. It then reads the answer from the last three digits of ⌊z⌋. The trick is to choose the magnification so that three digits are enough.

Remember

A positive double is w = fc · 2e with an integer significand fc < 253. Its neighbours are (fc ± 1) · 2e.

A parser rounds every decimal between the two midpoints to w. So the rounding interval is [w − 2e−1, w + 2e−1], and its width is exactly 2e.

A decimal exactly on a midpoint is a tie. Ties go to the even significand, so the interval includes its ends if fc is even and excludes them if fc is odd.

Powers of two are the exception. There the lower neighbour is only half as far away; see below.

Explorable · six frames

The window

Pick a double and step through the frames. Each frame magnifies further until only a 1000-unit window around z is left.

Why 100 to 1000?

Multiplying by 10k with k = 2 − ⌊e · log10 2⌋ turns the width 2e into δ = 2e · 10k = 102 + frac(e·log102), which always lies in [100, 1000). This magnification guarantees two things:

  • δ < 1000: the interval contains at most one multiple of 1000. If it contains one, that multiple is the shortest candidate.
  • δ ≥ 100: the interval contains at least one multiple of 100. So when no multiple of 1000 fits, the multiple of 100 nearest the centre is the answer, with one more digit.

The only multiple of 1000 that can be inside is 1000 · ⌊z/1000⌋, the last one at or below z. Its distance below z is r plus the fractional part of z, where r = ⌊z⌋ mod 1000. So the test “is that multiple inside?” becomes r < δi, where δi = ⌊δ⌋. Both are integers, so this is one integer comparison.

r < δi ⇒ output ⌊z/1000⌋ · 10−k+3

Trailing zeros of ⌊z/1000⌋ are then stripped (0.1 comes out as 1015 · 10−16 first). Otherwise the centre is y = z − δ/2. Rounding it to the nearest multiple of 100 gives the next digit, again using only integers.

dist = r − ⌊δi/2⌋ + 50,   digit = ⌊dist/100⌋

The output is (10 · ⌊z/1000⌋ + digit) · 10−k+2. It never ends in 0: a multiple of 1000 inside the interval would already have been found. Both formulas drop the fractional parts of z and δ. That is harmless except when the integer comparison is too close to call. Those cases are in the next section.

Deep dive · optional

Rare cases

The fog of fractions: when integer parts are not enough

z and δ are known only through their integer parts ⌊z⌋ and δi. Each one carries up to one unit of “fog”, its unknown fractional part. The lower end x = z − δ therefore lies somewhere in a 2-unit band around ⌊z⌋ − δi. The same is true of the centre y.

Usually the band is far from the tick being tested, and the fog doesn't matter. Only three situations need more information. None of them needs a full product; the answer is one bit.

Why a parity bit is enough. The tick being tested, 1000 · s, is even. When r = δi, ⌊x⌋ must be either 1000 · s or 1000 · s − 1, and these have different parities. The parity of ⌊x⌋ sits in the low bits of the product (2fc − 1) · φk, which compute_mul_parity computes without the top word. The same argument works for the centre y and the product 2fc · φk. The same call also reports whether the value is an exact integer, which settles closed ends and exact ties.

Explorable · why three digits

The κ knob

The “2” in k = 2 − ⌊e · log10 2⌋ is a parameter the paper calls κ. At κ = 0 the width lies in [1, 10). That is Schubfach's magnification, with coarse ticks every 10 and fine ticks every 1. Each extra step of κ magnifies ten times more, so the fog of one unit shrinks tenfold relative to the ticks. Turn the knob to watch the same double at each magnification.

So why stop at κ = 2? The code multiplies u = (2fc + 1) · 2β by the 128-bit cache entry, and u must fit a 64-bit register. 2fc + 1 needs 54 bits, and the shift β grows with κ, reaching ⌊(κ + 1) · log2 10⌋. The paper states the condition as p + 2 + β < 64 with p = 52. κ = 2 is the largest value that satisfies it (at most 63 bits); κ = 3 would need up to 67 bits.

Relation to Schubfach. Schubfach works at the κ = 0 magnification, with width in [1, 10). The integer parts are too coarse to decide there, so it computes x, y and z, each with its own 128-bit multiplication. Dragonbox uses the same kind of cached powers of ten, but magnifies by two more decimal digits. That leaves one multiplication for z, a shift for δ, and a parity product in well under 1% of inputs. The output is identical at every κ, because the coarse and fine grids are the same decimal grids. Only the integer arithmetic that finds the answer changes.

Special case · 2046 doubles

Powers of two

When the stored fraction is zero (fc = 252), the neighbour below is only half as far away as the neighbour above. The interval becomes [w − 2e−2, w + 2e−1], with width ¾ · 2e. Dragonbox handles this before the main path and “proceeds like Schubfach”. It scales so the width lies in [1, 10), with k = −⌊log10(¾ · 2e)⌋. Here fc is a power of two, so multiplying by it is just a shift of the cached constant. Both ends therefore come almost for free. The code tries multiples of 10 first; if none fits, it rounds the centre to the nearest integer.

One multiplication, three words

The cache entry φk is 10k scaled to a 128-bit integer. Multiplying it by the 64-bit u = (2fc + 1) · 2β gives 192 bits. The top word is ⌊z⌋. The middle word is the start of z's fraction: if it is zero, z is an integer. The low word is discarded. δi needs no multiplication at all: it is the cache's high word shifted right by 63 − β.

Sidebar: branchless trailing-zero removal

On the coarse path the significand can end in zeros (0.1 comes out as 1015 · 10−16). Upstream removes them without a division loop. It binary-searches 8, 4, 2 and 1 zeros. Each test multiplies by the modular inverse of 5n (mod 264), rotates right by n bits, and compares with ⌊264/10n⌋ + 1. The result is below that bound exactly when the significand is divisible by 10n, and then it equals the quotient. Four multiplications remove up to 15 zeros. (Idea credited upstream to reddit users r/pigeon768 and r/TheoreticalDumbass; see rtz_benchmark.)

Sidebar: full cache or compact cache

The full cache stores φk = ⌈10k · 2127 − ⌊k·log210⌋⌉ for k = −292 … 326. That is . The compact policy keeps every 27th entry plus the powers 50 … 526, in total. It recovers an entry by multiplying the base entry by 5offset, shifting, and adding 1. A recovered entry can be slightly larger in its last bits than the full table's. Both policies give identical output; this page's tests check that on random doubles and every power of two.

Sidebar: policies
  • sign: return the sign or ignore it.
  • trailing_zero: remove (default), remove_compact, report, or ignore.
  • decimal_to_binary_rounding: which parser the output must round-trip through. nearest_to_even is the default and gives the interval above. Other nearest modes change which ends are included. The directed modes (toward zero, toward ±∞, away from zero) use separate one-sided routines.
  • binary_to_decimal_rounding: the tie rule on the fine path (to_even by default, to_odd, away_from_zero, toward_zero). do_not_care skips the “closest” step: still shortest and round-tripping, but not necessarily closest.
  • cache: full or compact, as above.
