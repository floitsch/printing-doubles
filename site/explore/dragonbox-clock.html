The 1000-hour clock

Dragonbox zooms in until the rounding window is between 100 and 1000 units wide. After that, only the last three digits of one number matter. Wrap the number line around a clock with 1000 marks, and the algorithm asks one question: does the window cover 12 o'clock?

This approach turns the three variables of the real code (r, deltai and dist) into clock geometry. A short ruler section leads in. The clock carries the explanation, including the rare edge cases.

1 · Recap

Every double owns a window

A double w stands for every real number that would round back to it. Those numbers form an interval: from halfway to the lower neighbour, to halfway to the upper neighbour. We want the shortest decimal inside that window. If there are several, we want the one closest to w.

Remember

A positive double is w = fc·2e, with an integer significand fc < 253. Its neighbours are usually w ± 2e, so the window is [(fc − ½)·2e, (fc + ½)·2e], exactly 2e wide.

Under round-half-to-even, the endpoints belong to the window only when fc is even. A decimal exactly on the midpoint rounds to the neighbour with the even significand.

The exception is a power of two (fc = 252). Its lower neighbour is twice as close, so its window is lopsided. Section 8 covers this case.

2 · The on-ramp

Zoom until the window is 100 to 1000 wide

Multiply everything by 10k. The window's ends become x (left) and z (right), its centre becomes y, and its width becomes δ = 10k·2e. Dragonbox picks the one k that puts δ in [100, 1000):

k = 2 − ⌊e·log102⌋

The 2 is Dragonbox's constant κ. At this zoom, draw two sets of ticks on the ruler: a big tick at every multiple of 1000 and a small tick at every multiple of 100. The width guarantees two things:

  • δ < 1000, so at most one big tick fits. A big tick inside the window is a decimal that ends in at least three zeros. It is the shortest candidate, and it is the only one at that length.
  • δ ≥ 100, so at least one small tick fits. If no big tick fits, the small tick nearest the centre y is inside the window. It has one more digit than a big tick.

That leaves no search. Either a big tick is inside, or the answer is the nearest small tick. Use the buttons to see what goes wrong at other zooms.

3 · The trick

Only the last three digits matter

Whether a big tick falls inside the window does not depend on the leading digits. Moving the window by exactly 1000 moves the ticks by 1000 too, so the picture stays the same. Only z mod 1000 matters. Wrap the ruler around a circle of circumference 1000: every multiple of 1000 lands on one spot (call it 12 o'clock), and the multiples of 100 become ten hour marks.

4 · The centrepiece

Read the clock

Dragonbox computes only z, rounded down to an integer zi. One division by 1000 splits it into a prefix s and a remainder r, the position of the window's right end on the clock. The width's integer part is deltai, so the window is the arc of length δ that ends at r.

  • Does the arc cover 12 o'clock? The arc reaches back past 12 exactly when r < deltai. If it does, s·1000 is inside the window. Strip trailing zeros from s and you have the shortest output.
  • If not, pick the hour mark nearest the arc's middle. The middle is at about r − deltai/2. Adding 50 and dividing by 100 rounds it to the nearest hour: dist = r − ⌊deltai/2⌋ + 50, and the extra digit is ⌊dist/100⌋. The output is s followed by that digit.

Try the 0.3 family: from 0.3, press next double. The arc moves forward by δ ≈ 555 each time. It covers 12, misses, covers, misses: 0.3 → 0.30000000000000004 → 0.3000000000000001 → 0.30000000000000016. The second one is 0.1 + 0.2. Its arc runs from 166.5 to 721.6 and misses 12. The middle is at 444.1, the nearest hour mark is 400, and the extra digit is 4.

5 · Statistics

How often is 12 covered?

Neighbouring arcs tile the clock, so for a random double, the chance that its arc covers 12 is about δ/1000. For doubles in [1, 2), δ ≈ 222, so about 22% get the short output. Across all bit patterns, δ spreads log-uniformly over [100, 1000), and the rate averages to 0.9/ln 10 ≈ 39.1%.

Typical human input does much better. For normal doubles, the scaled centre y has 18 or 19 digits, so a big tick is a decimal with at most 16 significant digits. Any decimal you type with at most 15 significant digits is inside the window of the double it parses to, and it is a big tick. So such inputs always take the short branch. The fallback emits 16 or 17 digits.

6 · Edge cases

When an end lands exactly on a mark

The clock works with integer parts: r and deltai drop the fractions of z and δ. That can only mislead when an end of the arc is within one unit of 12, or when the middle is within one unit of halfway between two hour marks. The code detects exactly these cases (r == 0 with an integer z, r == deltai, and dist a multiple of 100) and looks closer. They are rare, but real numbers hit them. The magnifier next to each small clock shows ±3 units around the critical spot.

Why one parity bit is enough

When r == deltai, the integer parts say the left end x = z − δ is right at 12, but the fractional parts decide. Since ⌊x⌋ = zi − deltai + ⌊frac(z) − frac(δ)⌋, it is either 1000·s or 1000·s − 1. 1000·s is even, so the parity of ⌊x⌋ tells the two apart. Odd means x < 1000·s, and the tick is inside. Even means x ≥ 1000·s: the tick is inside only if x is exactly an integer and the left endpoint is included. Dragonbox gets that parity bit and an "is integer" flag from the low half of one more product, compute_mul_parity(two_fc - 1, cache, beta). It never computes x itself. The halfway test for y works the same way, with two_fc in place of two_fc - 1.

The smallest doubles, checkable by hand

At the bottom of the range, k = 326 and the scaled numbers have no prefix at all. For n·2−1074, the centre is y = 494.0656…·n and the window is y ± 247.03. The arc is 494 long and walks around the clock.

7 · Why it is fast

Only z is computed

The clock needs only r (from z) and deltai. Dragonbox computes zi with one multiplication of the shifted significand by a 128-bit cache entry φk, which is 10k normalised to 128 bits and rounded up. The same product's middle 64 bits tell whether z is an integer. δ does not depend on the significand at all: δ = 10k·2e, so deltai is the cache entry's high word shifted right. Schubfach, whose zoom idea Dragonbox builds on, computes x, y and z with three such multiplications. Dragonbox computes x or y only as a parity bit, in the rare cases from section 6.

Schubfach effectively uses κ = 0: a window width in [1, 10), with multiples of 10 as the short candidates.

        


        

          
8 · The exception

          
Powers of two do not fit the clock

          
When fc = 252 (and the exponent is not the smallest), the lower neighbour is only half as far away. The window [w − 2e−2, w + 2e−1] is lopsided. Its left half is half as wide as its right half. The clock's shortcuts assume y sits in the middle, so Dragonbox takes a separate path, which the source describes as "proceed like Schubfach". It picks k so that the width ¾·2e·10k is in [1, 10), and computes both ends directly from the cache. Since fc is a power of two, no real multiplication is needed. Then it checks for a multiple of 10 inside. If there is none, it rounds y up to an integer. This affects only 2046 doubles.

          
        


        

          
9 · Closing notes

          
Cache and policies

          
The cache. The table holds φk = ⌈10k·2127−⌊k·log210⌋⌉ for k from −292 to 326: 619 entries of 128 bits, about 9.9 KB. It is exact for 0 ≤ k ≤ 55. The compact cache policy stores only every 27th entry (23 entries) plus the powers 50 to 526 (27 64-bit words): 584 bytes instead of 9904. It rebuilds any other entry by multiplying the nearest stored entry below by a power of five, shifting, and adding 1. A rebuilt entry can differ from the full-table entry in the lowest bits, but the algorithm's error analysis covers that, and the output is the same.

          
Policies. Everything above uses the defaults: round-to-nearest-even for which decimals read back (this sets whether the window's endpoints count), round-to-even for ties between hour marks, trailing zeros removed, and the full cache. Other policies change small pieces. The window can use other nearest rules or the directed modes, which have their own simpler routines. The tie rule can be to-odd, away from zero, toward zero, or do_not_care, which skips the "closest" work and returns any shortest decimal. Trailing zeros can be left in or reported instead of removed, and the sign can be ignored.

          
Removing trailing zeros. On the short branch, s can end in many zeros (0.1 gives s = 1015). The real code strips them without division: it multiplies by modular inverses of powers of 5, rotates, and compares against a threshold, testing 108, 104, 102 and 101 in turn.
