The machine view

One double, start to finish

Żmij is Victor Zverovich's double-to-string library. This page explains the code as it is at tag v1.2 (commit d1682cb). The core has been rewritten several times, so older descriptions, including the 2025 blog post, describe a different design. The conversion has two halves. to_decimal turns the bits into a decimal significand and exponent. write turns those into characters. Both halves are straight-line integer code with no loops over digits.

Remember

A double is v = m · 2e, where m is a 53-bit integer (the leading 1 is implicit for normal numbers). Its neighbours are one ulp = 2e away, except that the gap below a power of two is only half an ulp.

Any decimal strictly less than half a gap away from v reads back as v. A decimal exactly half a gap away reads back as v only if m is even (ties go to even). The shortest output is the decimal with the fewest digits inside this rounding interval. If several have that many digits, the one closest to v wins.

Choose a number. Every figure below runs the pipeline on that number in your browser, using BigInt arithmetic that matches the C++ bit for bit.

Stage 1 · the design choice

Move the decimal point one place

Schubfach-family algorithms all start the same way. Pick a decimal exponent k from the binary exponent so that one ulp is roughly 10k. Multiply v by a stored power of ten, then read decimal candidates off the product. They differ in how many products they need and where they put the decimal point.

Żmij reads its single product one decimal place further left than yy does. The candidate with one digit fewer, which yy has to dig out with a division by ten, is then simply the integer part. The digit that was pushed out comes back from the fraction, computed in parallel.

Where the idea came from

Schubfach (Raffaello Giulietti) multiplies three times: once for v and once for each end of the rounding interval. yy (Yaoyuan Guo, in yyjson) multiplies once and gets the half-ulp by shifting the table entry. xjb (Xiang JunBo) moved the scale to 10−k−1. That turned the decisions into one carry, one comparison and one rounded digit.

Żmij's decision core is xjb's: the same 9 bits of headroom, the same +1 for even significands, the same rounding constant 263 + 6 and the same fix for F = 262. It runs on yy-style power-of-ten entries, which are rounded down, where xjb's are rounded up. On top of that, Żmij adds a Lean proof and lane-parallel formatting.

The 2025 post “Faster double-to-string conversion” describes an earlier Żmij: a Schubfach port with Cassio Neri's single-candidate trick. That design is gone. A comment inside to_decimal in zmij.cc credits the 10−k−1 scaling to Xiang JunBo.

Stage 2 · to_decimal

Follow the bits

Now the actual machine words. Each step below is one or two lines of to_decimal, run on your number. Values are in decimal by default; tick “show hex” to see the words as the CPU holds them. Fractions are given in units of the short grid: 1 unit = 10k+1, and the 64-bit fraction word F stands for F / 264 of a unit.

Why the “≈” signs are safe

The table entry is rounded down, and the product is truncated twice: the low 64-bit word and the 9-bit tail are dropped. So the machine's F and h can come out a couple of units of 2−64 below the exact values printed next to them. Near a decision boundary, that could flip a flag. The +6 in the digit constant shifts the rounding point enough to cover the truncation. The Lean proof checks every binary exponent and shows that no regular double lands in the remaining gap. The rounding-interval picture behind these three tests has its own page: approach A, the dial.

Stage 3 · to_digits and write

Sixteen digits at once

The decision stage hands over three things: an integer I of 15 or 16 digits, possibly one extra digit, and an exponent. Turning them into characters is half of Żmij's speed. There is no digit loop. The digits come out of a fixed three-level tree, where each level splits every lane in two with one multiply. After that, the output is assembled with a handful of stores whose positions come from small tables.

This is the portable SWAR path, from Xiang JunBo. The SIMD builds compute the same tree with vector instructions. On SSE2/SSE4.1 (Tobias Schlüter), both 8-digit halves sit in one 128-bit register and are converted together. On NEON (Dougall Johnson), the split by 108 is a multiply by 0xabcc77118461cefd and a shift by 90, and the lane steps use vqdmulh. Only the instructions change; each level does the same splitting.

Byte order. The register, printed most significant byte first as above, reads in digit order. A little-endian store writes the least significant byte first, so the portable path applies bswap64 before storing. Big-endian machines skip it, and the SIMD paths fold the reversal into a shuffle.

Stage 4 · guarantees

Proved vs tested

Żmij's README says “formally verified in Lean”. That claim is accurate, but it covers a specific scope, set out below.

PartStatusWhat exactly
Regular binary64 to_decimal (normals that are not powers of two, and all subnormals)provedLean 4 + Mathlib, theorem Zmij.correct in test/Zmij.lean (on top of test/Core.lean). After trailing zeros are removed, the output is the shortest decimal that reads back, and it is correctly rounded. The per-exponent inequalities are closed by decide +kernel over every binary exponent.
Caveat: the theorem is about a Lean model of to_decimal written by hand. The model includes the truncated table, the truncated product, the +6 and the 262 fix. No machine check shows that the C++ matches the model, and the C++ table generator is not checked either.
