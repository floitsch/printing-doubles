01

Introduction

Printing is a numerical conversion

A program does not store the characters “0.3” in a double. It stores one point selected from a finite binary grid. Producing source text, JSON, a log message, or a debugger value requires a second numerical operation: choosing a point from a decimal grid.

The grids do not coincide

Binary fractions have powers of two in their denominators; finite decimals have powers of two and five. Most familiar decimal fractions therefore lie between two binary64 values.

The original spelling is gone

The literals 0.3, 0.30, and a sufficiently long expansion can produce the same 64 bits. A formatter receives those bits, not the author’s notation.

The output has a contract

Exact expansion, fixed precision, correct rounding, recovery, and shortest recovery are distinct problems. The algorithms presented here do not all solve the same one.

The first task is therefore to see the two grids together. The instrument below places them on one exact coordinate system. It will recur throughout the algorithm chapters, where its highlighted intervals and candidates are driven by executable traces rather than hand-authored animation.

03

The actual problem

Which decimal should be printed?

The exact value stored for 0.3 is the finite decimal below. Printing it is correct, but it answers a question that most default formatters were not asked.

0.299999999999999988897769753748434595763683319091796875

Assume that a decimal parser rounds to the nearest binary64 value. The selected double then owns an interval of real numbers. Its lower boundary is halfway to the previous double; its upper boundary is halfway to the next double. Every decimal strictly inside that interval parses to the selected bits. At an exact midpoint, IEEE round-to-nearest, ties-to-even selects the neighbor whose integer significand is even. The stored significand of binary64(0.3) is odd, so both midpoint boundaries belong to its neighbors and are excluded from its interval.

Previous and next double are the adjacent representable binary64 values. There is no other double between either one and the selected value.

Lower and upper midpoint are the parsing boundaries halfway to those neighbors. An open marker says “excluded”; a filled marker says “included.”

Round-trip interval is the complete set of real values that parse to the selected double. A printer may choose any suitable decimal point in it.

1

Round trip

Reading the text must recover the exact original bits.

2

Use the fewest digits

No decimal with fewer significant digits may satisfy the same recovery requirement.

3

Choose the nearest

Among equally short answers, choose the decimal nearest the stored value, with a stated tie rule.

The shortest problem is geometric: find the coarsest decimal grid that still places a point inside the round-trip interval. Later algorithms differ chiefly in how they represent that interval, how they scale it, and how much arithmetic is required to prove that a candidate lies inside.

04

A forty-year relay race

How the representation changed

This is not one single benchmark. Early work solved several related output contracts: fixed precision, correct rounding, recovery, and shortest round trip.

1984

Coonen · Algorithms B, S, P, Q

Scale once, round once

Coonen showed that fixed-digit binary↔decimal conversion did not need enormous exact buffers. Use an extended format, estimate the decimal exponent cheaply, scale by a carefully rounded cached power of ten, and round to the requested digits. The result preserves recovery with tightly bounded error—but it is not a shortest-string algorithm.

1990

Steele & White · Dragon

Carry the boundaries along

Dragon generates digits left to right while tracking the exact rounding margins. It stops at the first digit that proves the output will round back correctly. Arbitrary-precision integers make it general and trustworthy, but comparatively expensive.

Gay · Burger & Dybvig

Make exact arithmetic practical

Gay’s dtoa and Burger–Dybvig retained the exact interval method while avoiding expensive work in common cases: better scaling, powers of ten, and small-integer fast paths. This family became the dependable fallback for later speculative algorithms.

2010

Loitsch · Grisu

A tiny floating point built from integers

Grisu replaces bignums with a 64-bit “diy-fp” and a cache of powers of ten. Grisu3 accepts only when its approximate interval proves the shortest, closest result; the 2010 paper reported success for about 99.5% of its tested binary64 inputs, with an exact fallback handling the rest.

Andrysco, Jhala & Lerner · Errol

More precision, then explicit corrections

Errol uses double-double arithmetic and a refined error analysis. Errol3 handles the remaining exceptional inputs with a generated bit-pattern table. The corrected paper reports 45 cases; the current public artifact contains 432, a discrepancy recorded in the chapter.

Adams · Ryū

Jump to the answer with fixed-size integers

Ryū proves that precomputed 126-bit power-of-five approximations are enough. It converts the interval with fixed-size multiplication, removes decimal digits, and needs neither a bignum nor a fallback. Its proof, portability, and open implementations drove broad adoption.

Giulietti · Schubfach; Jeon · Dragonbox

The same geometry, less work

Schubfach maps the rounding interval to a narrow integer interval and proves there are only a few candidates worth testing—no digit-removal loop. Dragonbox develops this family further with policy-driven caches and careful integer arithmetic.

05

State of the art · August 2026

The frontier is moving again

“Fastest” depends on CPU, compiler, data distribution, cache policy, and whether timing includes turning the significand into characters.

Established

Schubfach & Dragonbox

Mature, proven fixed-width algorithms. A 2026 cross-platform review found them generally ahead of Ryū across real datasets, with neither winning every workload.

New production contender

Żmij

A late-2025 Schubfach descendant that removes multiplications and branches and integrates faster digit formatting. Its library promises shortest, correctly rounded output and offers size-optimized tables.

New proof, new primitive

Unrounded scaling

Russ Cox’s 2026 construction uses a carry and sticky bit around a fast scaling primitive. The compact Go implementation covers printing and parsing; its proof is machine-checked in Ivy.

Experimental

Tejú Jaguá & xjb

Both report striking speed. Tejú’s reference repository still labels the core partial and its academic proof forthcoming; xjb is a 2026 preprint. They belong on the watchlist, not yet in the same evidence tier.
